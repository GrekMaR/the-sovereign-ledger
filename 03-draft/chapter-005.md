# Chapter 5: A Debt with Teeth

The clerk on the ladder licked his thumb, rubbed out the eight beside BARLEY, and chalked a nine.

Nobody on the Corn Hall floor cheered. A factor in a green coat swore under his breath and turned back to his tally-board. Two millers by the great scales stopped arguing about damp and started arguing about something worse. Grain dust hung in the high windows like smoke that had forgotten how to rise, and beneath it the hall smelled of sacking, sweat, and the lamp oil the night clerks burned when trading ran late. Trading ran late most nights now.

"Nine," Pell said at Lys's elbow. His stylus was already scratching the wax. "So eighteen thousand sacks at nine marks is—"

"Eight thousand one hundred crowns." Lys kept his eyes on the slate. "Counting the twelve hundred Dain paid us for."

"You're supposed to let me do it."

"You were slower."

"I was *checking*." Pell scowled through the gap in his teeth. "Eight thousand one hundred. Checked."

Five weeks without rain had done that. Through the south doors Lys could see the Harrowdale carters unloading, their wagons half-full, wheel rims caked with a pale dust that did not belong in a river city. Topsoil, leaving home. Mira had come back from the south six days ago with the same dust in the creases of her neck and a report she had delivered in four sentences: wells low, oats burned, seed held back, everyone asking when the rain they'd paid for was coming.

Eight thousand one hundred crowns. Against forty-one thousand.

In another life he would have called it a maturity mismatch and billed a client for the phrase. Here it came out plainer: the barley was finally worth something, and not enough. The sixty barges on the Saltreach, leased on Syndicate paper, were costing a hundred and twenty crowns a week to sit at their moorings and wait, and they would earn nothing until the Concord's houses were forced to bring grain upriver to cover their forward sales. That would not happen until Harvestmoon, or close to it.

The divine stay ran out on Emberwane 9. Thirty-seven days. After that the Tenth-Day Writ came back to life like something dug up, the Guild Court sat, Seat 39 went to House Dain, and the Charter Stone in Tallow Street unbound its master.

"We could sell some," Pell said, low. "Pay Dain a piece of it. Ask for time."

"Dain doesn't want money."

"Everyone wants money."

"Dain wants a Seat. If I hand him eight thousand crowns he'll thank me, write it in his book, and foreclose on the remaining thirty-three." Lys watched a woman at the bread-grain board count copper bits into a porter's palm for a quarter-measure of meal. She counted twice. The porter did not wait for the second count. "Anything short of the whole note is a gift."

He noticed he had turned the woman into a price point, and noticed that the noticing did not stop him. He filed that away with the other things he did not like about himself lately.

Veyra's courier had said *Korrath's Vault is going to make you an offer.* That had been a month ago. Korrath, it seemed, did not hurry.

* * *

The Oathkeeper's priestess found him at the Lesser Hammer three days later, in front of everyone.

Lys had climbed the Dawn Stair with Brother Oren for no better reason than to be seen climbing it. A guild that stopped coming to the Exchange was a guild the floor had already buried. So he stood at Seat 39 in his father's second-best coat while Sael's Hammer sold a blessing on a fishing weir and a tithe of lamp oil, and bid on nothing. Above the Seats the masked gods stood in their gallery, tall and still.

Between lots, the priestess walked out into the centre of the floor.

She was a slight woman in grey, older than her face suggested, and every finger of both hands wore a ring. They clicked faintly as she walked. That was the only sound. Forty Seats' worth of silk turned to watch her, and Lys felt the Concord's attention slide along the gallery and settle on him like dust on a ledge.

"Lysander Vey." She did not raise her voice; the Exchange carried it anyway. "Master of the Cinderwick Company, Seat Thirty-Nine. I am Sethra, Warden of the Vault of Vows. The Oathkeeper has watched your house keep its word to thirty-one villages. He offers your house credit."

Somebody in the Vantheil Seats laughed, briefly.

"That's generous," Lys said. "On what terms?"

"On his." Sethra's eyes were very calm. "Will you accept the Oathkeeper's credit, under vow, before this floor?"

Oren's sleeve brushed his. The steward's voice was barely breath. "Careful. If you refuse here, you refuse a god in front of forty Seats."

Lys understood that part without help. He also understood the other part: a man who agreed, in front of forty Seats, to an unread vow was a man who had signed a blank page. He had watched clients do it, once.

Across the floor, Ser Aurel Dain sat in House Dain's Seat with his hands folded on his cane. He looked interested in the way a man looks interested in weather over someone else's field.

"I don't vow to terms I haven't read," Lys said.

"Then the floor will remember that the Oathkeeper opened his hand to Cinderwick," Sethra said, "and Cinderwick looked at it."

It was a good line. She had not needed to rehearse it.

Lys chose his words the way Pell chose his coins, one at a time, looking at both faces. "Cinderwick accepts the Oathkeeper's invitation to terms. I'll come to the Vault today. I'll hear his offer and I'll answer it there, under his roof, with his witness."

A pause. Three heartbeats, long enough for a hammer to fall.

"Witnessed," Sethra said, and something at the corner of her mouth moved. She had got what she came for. He had given her less than she had asked and exactly what she needed: now, if he walked out of the Vault without a vow, the whole floor would know he had come to Korrath's table and left it.

He glanced up at the gallery. The Oathkeeper's tall masked figure had not moved at all. Lys had the unpleasant sense of being inventoried.

* * *

The Vault of Vows was cold in the way cellars are cold, a cold that had been stored.

It was a long hall cut back into the mountain's rock, pillared and vaulted, and every pillar was hung with rings. Iron, bronze, silver, a few of gold, threaded on cords from floor to ceiling, each one a vow held in pledge. The moment he crossed the threshold his Ledger-sight opened like a door kicked in.

Every ring had a thread.

Grey mostly, pending, taut, running out of the hall through the stone in every direction, toward debtors in the city and beyond it. Gold, some, the kept ones, softer and warmer. And here and there a red-black line that seemed to pull the light toward itself. The whole Vault was strung like a loom. Since the Stone had carried Cinderwick into the second tier he could see every witnessed contract in a room instead of only those under his hand, and he had thought that a gift. In the Corn Hall it was. Here it was a gale.

The nail behind his left eye went in to the head. He tasted copper and put the back of his hand to his lip and it came away red.

Sethra handed him a square of linen without breaking stride.

"Most bound masters find the Vault uncomfortable," she said.

"Most bound masters come often?"

"Only once."

She sat him at a stone table at the far end, under a lamp that burned emberstone and gave off almost no heat, and laid down a strip of vellum. Mira and Pell waited on the steps; the Vault admitted debtors and nobody else. Lys pressed the linen to his nose, narrowed his sight until the threads were a painful shimmer at the edges rather than a storm, and read.

Forty-five thousand crowns, in Vault drafts. Repayment of sixty thousand by the close of next year. Surety: the labour of the bound master of Cinderwick, his hands and his years, for so long as the debt stood unpaid.

He read it twice. It did not improve.

"This isn't credit," he said. "It's a purchase. You're lending against a man."

"The Oathkeeper lends against what is offered. Your warehouse is full of barley that no one wanted until last month. Your barges are leased, not owned. Your Seat cannot be pledged." Sethra folded her ringed hands. "You have a charter, and you have yourself."

"Then lend against the charter."

She waited.

He had built this argument over a dozen nights at the long table in Tallow Street. In his old life it had a name—a revenue-based facility, a lender taking a slice of future takings instead of a man's collateral—but names were no use here. The shape was what mattered.

"The Rain-Tithe returns rain to the Harrowdales in Mistmoon," he said. "When it does, thirty-one villages owe Cinderwick rain-rent for ten years. That's the asset. Not my back. Lend me fifty-five thousand crowns. I'll repay sixty-six by the end of next year." He held up a hand before she could answer. "Or, at the Vault's election, not mine, the Vault takes fifteen parts in every hundred of the Rain-Tithe's revenue for ten years instead. If the rain pays better than my coin, the Oathkeeper takes the rain. If it doesn't, he takes the coin. He chooses. I don't."

"You are offering Korrath a share in Ilvenne's weather."

"I'm offering him a share in thirty-one villages keeping their word for a decade. I'd think he'd like that better than a clerk with a bad back."

Sethra looked at him for a long time.

"Fifty-five," she said at last. "Sixty-six by the close of next year, or fifteen in the hundred for ten years, at the Vault's election. The Oathkeeper accepts the shape." She drew a second strip of vellum from her sleeve and laid it beside the first. "And his price."

The ink on it was dry. Days dry.

*If the price of bread-grain chalked at the Corn Hall of Vessant shall reach twelve marks the sack, the Cinderwick Company shall sell twenty thousand sacks at four marks the sack to the poor-houses of the city, delivery within the month, under the Oathkeeper's witness.*

Lys read it, and the room got colder.

"No," he said.

"It is not interest, Master Vey. It is the condition."

"Then change the rate. Seventy thousand by next year's end. Twenty in the hundred on the Tithe." He heard himself bidding against his own house and did not stop. "Raise the trigger to fourteen marks. Halve the sacks."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the Oathkeeper does not lend to a man who profits from hunger," Sethra said, "without binding that man to the hungry." She did not say it with any heat. She said it the way a clerk reads a clause. "You bought rain for a dry valley, and then you bought every barge on the river, and then you waited. Korrath is not a fool. Neither is the Weigher. You will be rich because the harvest failed. That is permitted. It is not free."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk up those steps with thirty-four days," she said, "and the floor learns why."

He thought about Mourngate: sixteen thousand eight hundred sacks on the floor, twenty thousand in the covenant. He thought about the woman counting bits for a quarter-measure of meal. And he thought, with a cold and exact part of himself, about the Stone in Tallow Street and what Unbinding did to a bound master, which Pell had described to him on the first morning in more detail than either of them had wanted.

A loan he could survive. The alternative, he could not.

"Witnessed," Lys said.

Sethra rose, came around the table, and closed her ringed hand around his left wrist.

The heat arrived slowly and then all at once. It went in rather than across, a wire drawn tight around the bone and heated from inside. He did not cry out. It cost him the feeling in his jaw. When she let go there was a band of fine dark lines around his wrist, close-written as a signature, and his skin was pink and shining around it.

In his sight a new thread ran out of the mark. Grey. Pending. It went through the stone wall and away toward the lower city, toward the poor-houses, and it thrummed faintly in time with his pulse.

"The Oathbrand will tell you when the covenant is at risk," Sethra said. "You will not need to ask it."

She counted out fifty-five thousand crowns in Vault drafts, sealed and corded, and put them in his hands, and the paper weighed almost nothing at all.

* * *

House Dain's counting room smelled of beeswax. Good pale wax that burned clean, and new ink, and the cold stone of an upper-city house old enough to have stopped needing to prove it. A window the height of two men looked down over the whole delta: the brown Saltreach, the roofs of the lower city stepping down to the wharves, and far off, the barges, sixty of them, tied up at Cinderwick's moorings in a neat grey row.

Lys suspected the desk had been placed so that he would see them.

Crell stood at Dain's shoulder with his ledger under his arm. Mira stood at Lys's. Pell stood a pace behind with the draft case clutched to his chest.

"Forty-one thousand crowns," Lys said. "The face of the note. In full."

Pell opened the case. Crell did not take it.

"The note specifies coin of the realm," Crell said.

"The note was witnessed under Korrath," Lys said. "These are Korrath's drafts. Every temple in Vessant takes them. If House Dain would like to explain to the Vault of Vows why the Oathkeeper's paper isn't good enough for its counting room, I'll wait."

Crell's face did nothing, but his fingers tightened on the ledger's spine.

"We will not refuse the Oathkeeper's paper, Crell." Aurel Dain's voice was mild and final. "Count it, please."

Crell counted. Lys let his sight open a hand's width, braced for it this time, and the room lit.

There was the note on the desk in its ribbon, and the thread that ran from it to Lys's own chest: grey, the colour of every bad morning since he had woken in this body. There were Crell's contracts, a tidy handful. And behind Dain, running out of a locked press against the wall, there were others, dozens of grey threads bundled tight together and running out through the window toward the river and the Corn Hall and the city beyond. Forward sales. Barley and bread-grain promised for delivery on Harvestmoon 1. Twenty-five thousand sacks, if the Syndicate's figure was right, and the Syndicate's figures usually were.

He could see that they existed. He could not see how they felt. Slack or straining, near breaking or sound: the second tier showed him the room, not which walls would fall.

He closed the sight before his nose could start again.

"Forty-one thousand," Crell said. "Correct."

Dain untied the ribbon himself, with long unhurried fingers, and held the note out across the desk. Lys took it. The moment it left Dain's hand the grey thread brightened, and brightened, and was gold. Kept. His chest felt lighter by exactly that thread; he had not known how hard it had been pulling.

"You've traded me for Korrath," Dain said. "I wonder if you know I was the gentler creditor."

"You bought that note from my father's bankers at thirty in the hundred," Lys said. "Twelve thousand three hundred crowns. You've just been paid forty-one. I think you were paid for your gentleness."

"I was paid for my patience. And for the risk that the heir of Harlan Vey would drink himself into the river before the Court could sit. The risk was real. I priced it." Dain's eyes rested on him with something like curiosity. "You have become very fond of prices, Master Vey. It's a change. People have remarked on it."

Lys did not let himself look at Mira. "People had a lot to remark on before."

"They did." Dain turned his cane a quarter-turn on the carpet. "May I tell you what I think you've misunderstood? Not as your creditor. I'm not that any longer."

"You'll tell me anyway."

"You look at the Exchange and see forty Seats, most of them held by seven houses, and you think it a kind of theft. It isn't. It's a kind of architecture." He gestured toward the window, the delta, the long drop to the water. "The Seats are few because the sky is small. There is only so much room up there, and every guild that climbs the Stair must be trusted not to bring it down. The Concord has held that trust for longer than anyone in this room has been alive. We have not always been kind. We have always been there."

"Your houses sold sixty thousand sacks forward at four marks," Lys said, "in a year you knew the Harrowdales had lost their rain."

It was the first time he had said it aloud to any of them. Crell went very still. Dain did not.

"Then you know we'll need barges," Dain said pleasantly, "and you'll rent them to us. Welcome to the architecture."

Lys held the note a moment longer. Mira was looking at the hearth; he could feel her looking at it, the small bright fire behind its brass guard. One step. The old Lys would have taken it, laughed, and made an enemy for the price of a flourish.

He handed the note to Pell. "File it."

Pell blinked, then put it in the case and closed the clasp, very carefully, as though it might still bite.

On the stairs down to the street, Mira said, "I'd have burned it in his fire."

"He'd have let you. Then he'd have told every house on the floor that Cinderwick is run by a boy who needs a bonfire."

"And instead?"

"Instead he has to tell them we paid in full with a god's paper and asked for a receipt." Lys flexed his left hand. The brand itched under his cuff like a healing cut. "That's a much duller story. It's harder to tell against us."

* * *

The remaining fourteen thousand crowns went faster than the forty-one.

The small creditors came to Tallow Street on the morning of Highsun 6 and queued down the steps into the street: a chandler's widow owed for two years of wick-cord, a rope-walk master with a bill Harlan had initialled and never paid, the cooper, the farrier, the ostler at the carters' yard, a moneylender's boy with a folded slip and a look of disbelief. Pell sat at the long table with the strongbox open and the ledger beside it, and read each name aloud before he paid it, the way he had read the books in the Temple forecourt. Six thousand two hundred crowns, in marks and crowns changed at the temple that morning, went out across the table one debt at a time. Lys shook every hand. A few of them held on longer than he expected.

The chandler's widow looked at the coin, then at him. "Your father said you'd pay one day."

"Did he?"

"No," she said. "But I thought it might be nice for you to hear."

By noon Hobb had masons on the roof at Mourngate, replacing the slates the thieves had broken and the ones that had simply given up, and a gang of warehousemen scrubbing the oil-stained floor with lye until the stone showed grey again. By evening timber was stacked for a second loft along the river wall, and Hobb was limping the aisles on his bad knee like a man who had waited five months to be given something to build.

That night the Charter Stone in the guildhall brightened without anyone touching it.

* * *

Lys did the covenant arithmetic at midnight, alone, with one tallow candle and the brand throbbing under his cuff.

He did it the long way, in ink, because he wanted to see it written.

At twelve marks the sack, twenty thousand sacks were worth two hundred and forty thousand marks. Sold at four, they fetched eighty thousand. The gap was a hundred and sixty thousand marks. Eight thousand crowns. That was what the famine covenant would cost Cinderwick the moment the clerk on the ladder chalked a twelve, and it was the least it could cost; the slate would not stop at twelve simply because Korrath's clause did.

Then there was Mourngate. Sixteen thousand eight hundred sacks on the floor. The shortfall was three thousand two hundred, and every one of those would have to be bought on the open market at whatever the market was charging that week, and sold to the poor-houses at four. Bought high to sell low, by divine order, in a hall full of men who would know exactly why he was buying.

He had made his whole plan on a failed harvest. Every mark on the slate was margin on the barley and a week closer to the day the Concord would come to him for barges. And he had just vowed, before a god, that when the price climbed high enough to mean real hunger, he would hand a year's profit to the hungry.

He was betting on the famine and sworn to feed it. The two positions did not cancel out. They sat on either side of his left wrist and pulled.

Somebody knocked at the street door.

It was a child, eight or nine, with a paper lantern on a stick and a tin pin shaped like a lantern at her collar. She held out a folded note, waited to be sure he had taken it, and was gone up Tallow Street before he could find a bit to give her.

The note was not signed. It did not need to be.

*Twelve is such a round number. You'll want to buy your three thousand two hundred early, before the rest of the city learns to count. — V.*

Lys stood in the doorway with the note in his hand and the night air cold on the brand.

Twelve marks. Twenty thousand sacks. His shortfall to the sack. None of it had been said on the Exchange floor. The covenant had been written in ink days dry before he ever sat down, and a month ago Veyra had told him to accept whatever Korrath offered.

She had known the price. She had known it before he did, and she had let him walk into the Vault believing he was the only one at the table who had read ahead.

He went back inside, sat down at the ledger, and under the eight thousand crowns he wrote a second line, in smaller hand, that he did not yet know how to price:

*Owed to V. — unknown.*

The brand on his wrist kept time beside his pulse, patient as a creditor.
