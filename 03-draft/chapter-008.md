# Chapter 8: The Blessing License

The delivery bell at the Corn Hall rang at the fourth hour before dawn, and House Dain's wagons were still coming in when it stopped.

Lys counted them from the weighing-yard steps. Eleven wagons in a neat line, then a gap, then three more that took the cobbles too fast, tailboards rattling on their chains, teamsters looking at their horses and not at the clerks. Lanterns hung from iron hooks along the yard wall. Grain dust turned in the light like flour in a bakehouse, and under it lay the smells of wet sacking, horse, and lamp oil burned low.

Crell stood at the tally-table with his ledger open. His pen kept moving after the bell's last note. He turned a page as if nothing had happened.

"House Dain delivers," the yard-reeve called, a broad man with a chalk-stick behind each ear. "Against forwards sold under Korrath's witness: twenty-five thousand sacks at four marks. Sacks tallied at the bell—" He bent over Crell's ledger. The yard went quiet enough to hear a horse shift its weight on the stones. "Eleven thousand, two hundred."

Someone at the rail laughed, a short, ugly sound, and stopped.

Then everyone was talking. Millers with flour worked into the seams of their coats. A biscuit-baker who had promised hardtack to the river crews. A chandler's widow in a black shawl who had bought forty sacks forward and sold them already as loaves she had not yet baked. They pressed toward the table, and Crell's porters stepped in front of it without being told.

Lys let the Bind open a finger's width.

The yard lit up with thread. Grey lines ran from Crell's ledger to every buyer at the rail, forwards still pending under Korrath's witness, and as he watched, the grey flushed dark. The strain crawled along each thread like damp through plaster, and where it reached the buyers it did not stop. It jumped. From millers to the bakers they had promised flour, from bakers to the taverns and barracks they had promised bread, fine red-black cracks spreading through the yard gate into the dark streets, a map of every promise that had rested on Dain's.

The nail went in behind his left eye. He closed the Bind before the blood came and pressed a knuckle to his lip anyway, out of habit. Dry. Good.

"Thirteen thousand eight hundred short," Pell said at his elbow. The boy had the Corn Hall's own slate under his arm, the top line still wet. BARLEY — 14. "At fourteen marks that's nine thousand six hundred and sixty crowns to buy them in."

"If there were thirteen thousand eight hundred sacks in the city to buy."

"There's grain coming down the Saltreach," Pell said. "On our barges. Into our sheds."

Mira, on Lys's other side, made a sound through her nose that might have been approval. "And he's too proud to rent either."

"Then he'll borrow to cover the gap," Lys said.

Crell closed his ledger, tucked it beneath his arm, and crossed the yard, unhurried, the crowd parting for him like water for a barge. He stopped one step below Lys, which put their eyes level.

"Master Vey. You'll be climbing this morning."

"So will your master."

"My master is always climbing." Crell glanced at the slate under Pell's arm, then at the chandler's widow, who had sat down on an empty grain sack and was staring at nothing. "Enjoy the Stair. It is the last morning you will climb it with nobody counting your purse."

He went past them into the street. A child with a paper lantern pinned to her collar slipped past him going the other way and pressed a folded draft into his hand. Syndicate paper, heavy and cream-coloured, sealed with smoked wax.

Twenty-five thousand, two hundred crowns, payable at the Temple of the Balance before first light. The shadow notes had paid. Every sack Dain had failed to put on the scale was, somewhere in Veyra's ledgers, a coin moving toward Tallow Street.

Lys put it in his coat. The chandler's widow was still sitting on her sack. He did her arithmetic too, without meaning to, forty sacks at the gap between four marks and fourteen, and was already walking before he had finished it.

* * *

Brother Oren had the temple scales uncovered by the time they reached Scale Street. Two brass pans the width of cartwheels hung from a beam older than the building; between them a glass cup waited on its own small plinth.

"Twenty-five thousand two hundred," Oren said, reading the draft through ink-stained fingers. "At the temple's rate of a hundred and twenty to the gleam—"

"Two hundred and ten."

"I was going to say it." He did not smile. He never did before sunrise. "Two hundred and ten gleams to the Cinderwick Stone, on top of what the poor-houses gave you. Six hundred and twelve in all. That is more grace than your father saw in his life, and you are going to carry it up a staircase and hand it to a god before breakfast."

"Only if I win."

"That," Oren said, "is what worries me."

The coin went into the western pan in sealed sacks. The eastern pan held nothing. Oren spoke the conversion, the words dry and old, and the empty pan sank as if something heavy had been set in it. Light gathered in the glass cup, thin as the skin on warm milk, then drained away through the plinth toward Tallow Street, where the Stone would be brightening in an empty hall with only old Hobb to see it.

Pell stayed to witness the tally. Mira and Oren came with Lys to the back court, where the flagstones were already paling.

The Dawn Stair did not rise. It was simply there, between one breath and the next, white steps going up into grey cloud, each lit from inside like alabaster over a candle. His legs still did not believe it.

Oren read the lot notice aloud as they climbed; he had never met a document with the Scale stamped on it that he did not want to hear. Lot 3 was the one that had kept Lys awake.

"The Weigher's License. Sael's blessing to keep a counting-house in Vessant under the Scale's witness, and to issue notes of hand which every temple of the Pantheon will accept as backed by grace." He lowered the notice. "It has not been offered since before your father was born. The last house to hold it let it lapse rather than pay the tithe."

A bank, said the part of him that was still Theo. A bank with a god for its regulator and every temple in the city for a branch. Paper that was good as grace at any altar was good as grace in any market, because nobody would refuse a note they could walk to Scale Street and lay on the stone. Whoever held it could make money out of ink and faith.

"Dain wants it," Mira said.

"Every house wants it." Oren tucked the notice away. "Only one of them spent the night borrowing."

* * *

The Exchange was cold the way deep water is cold. Marble ran out in every direction and ended in cloud, no wall, no rail, only the gallery of forty carved Seats curving around the floor like the rim of a bowl. Sunlight came from below, pink on the undersides of things. The gods stood at the centre, taller than any man should be, masked in bronze and horn and polished wood. Sael's mask was a plain disc of silver with two narrow slits for eyes. The Hammer rested in a hand that never seemed to move.

Seat 39 was near the end of the gallery, a high-backed stone chair carved with a cinder and a wick. Lys sat. Mira stood behind him. Oren took the liturgist's stool at his left.

Across the curve of the bowl, Ser Aurel Dain sat in dove-grey silk, speaking pleasantly to the head of House Vantheil. The Castellane and Ruske Seats were bunched close on his other side. Oskel, Merrow, and Hale sat a little apart now, watching Dain the way men watch a neighbour's roof when the chimney is on fire.

Lys opened the Bind.

It cost more up here, a hot wire from eye to jaw. But the threads were plain. From Vantheil, from Castellane, from Ruske, grey lines ran to Dain's Seat, fresh and thick, each knotted with Korrath's seal. Grace lent against Dain's word a few hours ago. Each thread already carried a dark bloom at the Dain end, where it tied to a house that had failed thirteen thousand eight hundred sacks since midnight.

He closed his eyes and the threads went out. Blood touched his upper lip. Oren passed him a handkerchief without looking at him.

Fishing rights and a toll ford went quickly. Then the herald in grey called the third lot, and the gallery went still.

"Lot three. The Weigher's License. Bid bonds are posted. The floor opens at one hundred gleams."

"One hundred," said Merrow.

"One hundred fifty," said Hale.

It climbed in fifties, then in twenties, the old rhythm of the Exchange: a call, a pause, a call. Oskel came in at two hundred and sixty and left at three hundred, Lady Benedetta setting down her fan as if it had grown heavy. Merrow held to three-forty. Dain had not yet spoken.

"Three hundred and sixty," Lys said.

"Four hundred," said Ser Aurel Dain, the first word he had spoken, and smiled across the bowl as if they had met at a garden party.

Mira bent to Lys's ear. "How far can you go?"

"Six-twelve."

"And him?"

"Further. He's got Vantheil and two others in his pocket."

"Then think about what you're bidding for." "You win this, and you're sitting on the only counting-house the gods will vouch for. Every note that passes through it, you shave a sliver off. A gleam in a hundred. Nobody feels it. In ten years you'd have more grace than the Concord ever had."

"Four-twenty," Lys said to the crier, and to Mira, without turning, "Go on."

"The Vault wants sixty-six thousand crowns by the end of next year. Every house here watched you empty your storehouse into the poor-houses at four marks." She paused while Dain raised to four-sixty. "You'll need teeth. The skim is teeth."

"And what does it make him?" Oren said quietly.

Mira did not look at the priest. "Alive."

"It makes him a tick." Oren's ink-creased hands stayed folded on his knee. "A counting-house under the Scale is a well that the whole city drinks from. You could sit at the bottom of it and take a mouthful from every cup. Or you could lend. Put notes in the hands of carters who can't afford a second wagon, farmers who need seed before the rain comes, dockers who want a boat of their own. The first way makes Cinderwick fat. The second makes Vessant bigger."

"The second way," Mira said, "gets a farmer in the Harrowdales a loan he can't repay after a bad season, and it's our paper he defaults on."

"Then lend with care."

"Care." She said it the way she might have said *rain* in a dry month. "You don't ride the valleys, Brother. I do."

"Five hundred," said Dain.

Lys watched the herald chalk it and felt his mind do what it did when he was afraid. It sorted. It stacked. The Korrath debt went in one column, the Concord's grudges in another, and the skim sat in the middle, clean and quiet and easy to understand. A gleam in a hundred on a city's worth of paper. He could see the ledger page already. It had no faces on it. That was what made it so easy to read.

"Five-twenty," he said.

"Five-sixty."

"Six-twelve."

Everything he had. The herald chalked it. For one breath the gallery was his.

"Six hundred and fifty," said Ser Aurel Dain.

Silence opened across the floor. The first heartbeat began.

Lys looked at the gods.

He had been looking at them all morning without seeing them. They had not moved during the bidding. Not when Merrow opened, not when Oskel folded, not when Dain put six hundred and fifty gleams on the table. The silver disc of Sael's mask had not so much as tilted.

His left wrist itched under the cuff. The Oathbrand, quiet since the covenant was paid.

Korrath had not wanted a lump sum either. The Vault had taken fifteen parts in a hundred of the Rain-Tithe as readily as sixty-six thousand crowns, then added a covenant that had to be kept in public, cart by cart. Ilvenne had sold him rain for a hundred and sixty-four gleams and twenty a month. Oskel had held that tithe before him and lost it the season her payments stopped.

The price at the hammer was not the price. It never had been. Grace was kept promises made measurable, and a heap of gleams on the table was one promise kept once. The gods did not want a bucket. They wanted a well.

Annuity against lump sum, said the part of him that had once sat in glass towers pricing other men's debts. Every house on this floor has been bidding the wrong number for a hundred years.

The second heartbeat.

Lys stood up.

"Cinderwick offers terms."

The herald's chalk stopped against the slate. A murmur went round the gallery, Merrow's steward half out of his Seat, someone in Castellane's colours laughing. Oren was already on his feet beside Lys, voice pitched to carry, dry as a clause.

"By the old rule of the Scale, set down at the founding of the Exchange: a bidder may offer terms, and the Weigher may weigh them. The rule has never been struck."

"Terms," said Ser Aurel Dain from his Seat, without rising, without any heat, "are what men offer when they cannot pay."

"Terms are what men offer when they intend to keep paying," Lys said. He did not look at Dain. He looked at the silver mask. "Three hundred gleams now. And one gleam in every twenty of the face of every note the Cinderwick counting-house issues, to the Scale, for as long as the license stands."

He heard Mira stop breathing behind him.

"The notes will be backed by goods under Cinderwick's hand. The grain in Mourngate and in Low Wharrow. Freight on sixty barges of the Saltreach. The rents of the Rain-Tithe when the rain comes back to the Harrowdales. And they will be lent to the people who move those goods, the carters and the farmers and the dockers, because paper that sits in a strongroom earns the Scale nothing." He drew breath. The nosebleed was starting again; he let it. "House Dain offers six hundred and fifty gleams, once. Cinderwick offers you a share of every honest promise this city writes on paper, for as long as we keep them."

Nobody spoke.

The silver mask turned.

It moved no faster than the shadow on a sundial, and every eye in the gallery followed it around the bowl until the two narrow slits faced Seat 39. Lys felt the regard like sunlight through a lens, cold rather than hot. Something enormous and patient, reading his terms the way he read a balance sheet, looking for the line where the numbers lied.

One heartbeat. Two.

On the third, Sael spoke. The voice was a bell rung under water, and it came from nowhere and everywhere.

"Weighed."

The Hammer fell.

The sound went up through the soles of Lys's boots. Then the gallery broke, not into shouting but into a long indrawn gasp, forty Seats' worth of breath going in at once, and after it a rustle of heads turning. Not toward him. Toward the other masks: Ilvenne's straw-pale gold, Korrath's iron, the heavy dark horn at the edge of the floor that belonged to the god who sold only below. Every lord and proxy in the gallery was looking at the gods as if for the first time, and doing sums.

He had just told the whole Exchange what its masters ate.

* * *

The herald brought the charter scroll on a silver tray. Lys took it bare-handed.

He did not need to open the Bind; it opened for him. A gold thread ran from the scroll into his palm and out across the floor to the silver mask, thick as a bell rope, humming. Beside it, thinner and new, a dozen grey threads spun out from his wrist toward nothing he could see yet: every note not yet written, every loan not yet made, pending. Promises he now owed a god in advance.

The pain folded him forward over the scroll. Oren's hand caught his shoulder.

"Breathe," the priest said. And then, lower, for him alone: "You chose the well."

"I chose the one that pays better."

"Did you," Oren said. It was not a question.

When Lys could stand, Mira was studying him, scarred brow drawn down. "You bid three hundred against his six-fifty," she said, "and won."

"I bid more than he did. I just didn't ask Sael to take it all at once."

"And you've tied every note we'll ever issue to lending it out."

"Yes."

"No skim."

"A gleam in twenty goes to the Scale. If I take a gleam in a hundred on top, the Scale will know it. It's watching every note." He wiped his lip with Oren's ruined handkerchief. "The teeth are in the terms, Mira. Nobody on this floor can outbid a god's own interest."

She nodded once, the way she nodded at a river crossing she had judged fordable.

Veyra was waiting at the head of the Dawn Stair, where the proxies stood when they were not bidding. Her smoked-glass spectacles caught the pink light from below and gave nothing back. She was smiling.

"Master Vey. I have spent twenty years in the dark under this staircase, selling secrets smaller than the one you just gave away for nothing."

"Not for nothing. For three hundred gleams and a tithe."

"For nothing," she repeated gently. "By the next Dawn Auction every house with a Seat will come up those steps with terms in its pocket. Some of them will write better terms than yours."

"Then I'll have to keep better promises than theirs."

"Yes." She tilted her head, and the amusement thinned enough for something more careful to show through. "I bet on you in the spring because I thought you were clever. I did not know you would change the rules of the room while we were all still sitting in it. My twenty parts in a hundred of the barges looks very small this morning."

"It's the same size it was yesterday."

"That," said Veyra, "is exactly what I mean." She went down the Stair ahead of them without saying goodbye.

Ser Aurel Dain passed them at the first landing. He walked alone; the Vantheil and Castellane stewards were well ahead of him, heads together.

"Congratulations, Master Vey. You have taught forty houses what the gods are hungry for." His voice was as mild as ever. "I wonder whether you have considered that they will now all come to the table at once, and that the table was set for fewer."

"The sky's not as small as you think, Ser Aurel."

"No," Dain said. "Perhaps it is smaller." He inclined his head and went on down.

Lys opened the Bind a crack and watched the grey threads of borrowed grace trail after him down the steps. Every one of them had gone red-black along its whole length.

* * *

The guildhall on Tallow Street smelled of old tallow and new ink, and the Charter Stone was burning.

Not with fire. The vein of light that had run through the grey block like a crack in a cellar wall had widened until it lit the rafters, and Hobb sat on a barrel beside it with his bad leg out straight and his hat in his hands, as if in church. Pell had pushed the long table up to the Stone and was copying the readout into the day-book with his tongue between his teeth.

"Thirteen thousand six hundred," Pell said, not looking up. "It was seven thousand nine hundred at noon yesterday. It's gone up in jumps all afternoon, every time somebody comes to the door."

People had been coming to the door since they got back. Two carters from the Harrowdale road, asking whether the counting-house would lend against a second wagon. A cooper who wanted to know whether the temple would really take the new notes. Garrow Finch had sent a docker with four words: *Brotherhood wants boats. Talk.*

There had been others. A Merrow steward, very polite, asking when Master Vey kept office hours. Two men who said they were wool factors and whose boots had never been near a sheep. The Stone could count grace. It could not count enemies. Lys had started that ledger in his head on the Stair and had not finished it.

Mira leaned in the doorway, watching the street. "Yesterday we were only rich," she said.

"Yesterday nobody was watching."

She almost laughed.

The door banged. Oren came through it too fast for a man his age, cassock dusty to the knee, face grey above it. He held a notice torn from a temple board, the nail hole still in the top. The paper was black, the ink the dull red of emberstone before it catches.

"This went up on every temple in the city an hour ago," he said. "All at once. Nobody saw who nailed them."

Lys took it. The paper was warm.

*By the will of Maruth Below. The concession of emberstone in the Cindermaw, held by House Dain these thirty years, comes to renewal at the Deep Hammer at the mouth of the Cindermaw on the fifteenth of Harvestmoon. Lots at the Deep Hammer are sold only to those who have stood in the dark.*

"Stood in the dark," Mira said from the doorway. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the Deepway," Oren said. "Down to the first gate and back, on your own feet. No proxies. No stewards. Maruth does not take a bidder's word that he has been below. He takes the bidder." He looked at Lys. "Dain has held that concession since before you were born. Every forge in the upper city, every ward-lantern on the walls, burns his emberstone. It is the last thing he has that is worth more than his debts."

Lys turned the notice over. Nothing on the back. The paper's heat was fading into his palm.

He thought of the threads trailing after Dain down the Stair, red-black from end to end. A man with that much borrowed grace falling due, and one lot left to bid on that could pay it all back.

"He'll pledge everything," Lys said. "His grace, his allies' grace. His Seat."

"His Seat," Oren agreed. "And whatever he has left that Maruth will count."

The Stone hummed beside them, bright enough to read by. Somewhere in the upper city, Lys supposed, the lamps in House Dain's counting room were being lit, and a man who never raised his voice was sitting down with his ledgers to work out what his house was worth to a god who lived under the mountain.

In fourteen days Lys would have to walk into the dark and bid against him for it.
