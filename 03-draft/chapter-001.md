# Chapter 1: The Bankrupt Guild

The hammering started before the bells.

Lys reached the guildhall door with a ledger still open against his chest and a candle stub drowning in its own wax. Four blows, a pause, then a fifth, lighter, to seat the nail. Somewhere in the smudged back rooms of this borrowed head, the old Lys had heard that rhythm before.

He pulled the bolt.

Crell stood on the step in a grey coat buttoned to the throat, a hammer in one gloved hand and a spare nail held between his lips the way other men held a pen. He removed the nail, returned it to a waistcoat pocket, and bowed to the exact depth owed a guildmaster. Not a hair more.

"Master Vey. You're up early."

"I'm up late." Lys looked past him at the door. The parchment hung square to the grain, pinned at the top by a single nail through a blob of blue wax. "You could have knocked."

"The writ knocks for me. That's rather the point of it." Crell tucked the hammer under the ledger he carried against his ribs. "Tenth-Day Writ, on behalf of Ser Aurel Dain, holder of the Cinderwick note. Forty-one thousand crowns, principal and accrued, payable on or before the thirteenth of Seedmoon. Failing payment, the Guild Court sits that morning to hear foreclosure. Any Seat held by the debtor passes to the creditor, the creditor being a chartered house." He paused, courteous. "Seat Thirty-Nine, in your case."

Behind him a carter's boy led two mules toward the river. Across the way a shutter opened a hand's width and stayed there.

"Your lord bought that note from my father's bankers at thirty in the hundred," Lys said. "Twelve thousand three hundred crowns. He's asking for forty-one."

"He's asking for what the note says."

"He doesn't want the money."

Crell's face did nothing at all, which was an answer of a kind. "My lord asked me to say he regrets the necessity."

"Does he?"

"He asked me to say it. I've said it."

Lys stepped down onto the stone and put two fingers against the parchment.

The world split along a seam.

A thread rose out of the blue wax — thin as a hair, bright as a struck match — and climbed. It was grey, the dull grey of a pending account, shot through its length with gold, and it did not stop at the eaves. It went up past the chimneys and through the brown dawn haze, and somewhere far above, beyond sight, it was *held*. He felt the holding the way you feel a rope go taut in the dark: something enormous and patient on the far end, keeping count.

Korrath's witness. The writ was valid. Of course it was valid.

Then the nail went in behind his left eye.

He took his hand away. The thread vanished. The street came back, and with it a warmth on his upper lip, and a dark coin of blood struck the step between his boots, then another.

"Here." Crell held out a folded handkerchief, clean and pressed. When Lys didn't take it, he laid it on the doorframe beside the writ. "The Bind, I expect. It takes some masters that way at first." He looked at Lys's face for a moment longer than courtesy required. "You're sober, Master Vey."

"Four days."

"My lord will find that interesting." Crell touched his hat. "Ten days. I'd count them."

* * *

The hall smelled of tallow. It always would. The Cinderwick Company had begun, three generations back, as chandlers who also owned carts, and the fat of ten thousand candles had soaked into the beams until the whole building smelled faintly of a temple after the lamps were snuffed. Harness hung on pegs along one wall; the counting desks along the other stood mostly bare.

At the far end stood the Charter Stone.

It came up to his waist — a block of grey stone, unpolished, veined through with a line of light like a crack in a shuttered lantern. For four nights he had sat across from it with the books, and for four nights the light had lain steady and faint, keeping him company.

Now it stuttered. The vein dimmed, flared, and then the light gathered itself on the stone's face and wrote.

```text
⟦ CINDERWICK COMPANY — CHARTER STONE ⟧
STANDING: 212 (Tier I — Tin Seat)
SEAT 39 — UNDER LIEN
GRACE: 9 gleams
```

A blanket moved under the second counting desk. Pell sat up, all elbows, hair crushed flat on one side, and saw the stone and was on his feet in the same motion.

"That's the lien," he said. "It came through. Sir. It's come through."

"I know." Lys pressed Crell's handkerchief — he'd taken it after all — against his nose and tipped his head back. The beams swam. "Dain's man nailed it up. We have until the thirteenth."

Pell looked at him, then at the blood, then back at the stone. He was fifteen and had a gap between his front teeth wide enough to whistle through, and he was the fastest hand with a column of figures Lys had seen in either of his lives. Four nights ago, the boy had watched Lys open the ledgers as if Lys had opened a vein.

"Pell. What happens to the master when the Court forecloses?"

"The Stone's struck, sir. Unchartered."

"And the master."

Pell's mouth worked. "You're bound. Your father bound you at the winter, before — before. That's how you see the oaths. That's what the blood is." He wiped his palms down his shirt. "When the Stone's struck, the Bind comes undone. Unbinding, they call it. There was a ropewalkers' guild went under when I was small. Their master was in the Court when they read the order. My aunt said he sat down on the floor, polite as anything, and he was dead before the bell." A swallow. "Some go slower. Most don't live."

Lys lowered the handkerchief.

He had spent four nights on the guild — what it owed, what could be sold, which creditors would stretch and which would sue. The kind of rescue plan he used to charge a fortune for in a glass tower beside another river. He had not once asked what the lien meant for *him*, because in his old life a company's failure had never been a cause of death for anyone but the company.

There was a faint pull under his breastbone. He had been taking it for fear. It sat there now, patient, like the far end of that thread.

Forty-one thousand crowns. Ten days. Or he sat down on a courtroom floor, polite as anything.

"Right," he said, almost level. "Find me the wage book."

* * *

Mira Thorne came at the third hour with three teamsters behind her, and she did not knock either.

A head shorter than Lys, she took up more of the room. A pale scar ran through her left eyebrow and split it neatly in two, which made one half of her face look permanently unconvinced. The teamsters stood at her shoulders, hats in hand, not meeting his eyes. She did.

"Four months' pay," she said. "Mine and theirs. Then we're gone, and you can drink the rest of the Company without us watching."

The memory came up through the smudge the way the others did — not a story, a picture. This hall, winter, Harlan Vey three days in the ground. Mira in a black coat with snow melting on her shoulders, asking the heir what the caravans should do. The heir had told her. Lys couldn't make out the words, only the shape of her face afterwards, and the fact that she had walked out without hitting him, which seemed in retrospect a considerable feat of restraint.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'll stand."

"Then stand here." He turned the ledger on the desk so it faced her, and opened it to the page he had ruled last night in Pell's careful ink. "I want you to read this before I say anything."

Her eyes narrowed. The half-eyebrow didn't move. But she came to the desk, because a caravan captain who couldn't read a manifest didn't stay a captain long, and she read.

He watched her find the numbers. Forty-one thousand to House Dain. Six thousand two hundred more, split among a dozen smaller names — the fodder merchant, the rope-maker, two tallow renderers, a smith who had shod every mule the Company owned and been paid for none of it since autumn. Wages owed to fourteen members: two hundred and sixty-eight crowns. Cash in the strongbox as of this morning: two hundred and twelve.

She read it twice.

"Your father," she said at last, "showed me the books once. They didn't look like this."

"He showed you the front of them." Lys tapped the page. "This is all of it. Nobody else in the city has seen that line. Not the Court, not Dain. Now you have."

"Why?"

"Because you're owed twenty-eight crowns and your men are owed thirty-two between them, and you have a right to know why you're not getting it."

One of the teamsters shifted. Mira didn't look round. "I can see the strongbox has two hundred in it."

"It does. If I pay you four, you walk out with sixty crowns, and the other ten who are owed the rest get a hundred and fifty-two to split. The fodder bill falls due on Sael's Day. So by Sael's Day the mules stop eating, and the Court sits on the thirteenth, and Dain takes the Seat and the hall and the carts, and every name in that column" — he ran his finger down it — "gets nothing, forever. You'd be the only four people paid by this Company again."

"Sounds fair to me."

"It's perfectly fair. It's also the end." He drew a sheet of paper from under the ledger. He had written it at the fourth hour, by candle, and then written it again because the first draft read like something from a world with printing presses. "So I'm offering something else. Not wages. A stake."

"A what."

"A share in the Company. Written, numbered, witnessed at the temple under the Balance. Everything Cinderwick earns from today, after its debts, you hold a part of. If it dies in ten days, the paper's worth nothing. If it lives, it's worth more than four months of anybody's wages." In his head the phrase was *debt-for-equity* and it came with a slide deck. Out loud it sounded like a drunk's promise wearing a clean shirt. He knew it. "I'd rather owe you a future than pay you a funeral."

Mira looked at the paper and didn't touch it.

"Last winter," she said, "I stood where you're standing and asked what I should do with forty mules and nine wagons and eleven drivers who'd followed your father through two floods. You told me to feed them to the dogs and send you the bill." Her voice didn't rise. "You don't remember."

"No," he said. It was true, and it was the only answer he had that wasn't a lie.

"And now you've stopped drinking and started talking like a counting-house, and I'm supposed to hand you four months of my life for a piece of paper."

"Yes."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It was not a smile. "You're either mad or someone's put you up to this."

"Come to Mourngate," he said. "Look at what we own. Then decide."

"I know what you own. I've hauled every sack of it."

"Then it won't take long."

She looked at him for a long moment, then jerked her head at the teamsters. "Wait outside. Don't let him near the strongbox." And to Lys, flat: "One hour."

* * *

Mourngate sat on the Saltreach bank like a stone animal lying down to drink. The river ran brown and slow beside it, thick with silt from the delta, and the air smelled of wet rope and mud and, underneath, a dry sweetish dust that caught at the back of the throat.

Hobb met them at the wicket gate, keys in hand, his bad knee locked straight so he could lean on it. He was old enough that his eyebrows had outgrown the rest of his face, and he greeted Mira by name and Lys by title, in that order.

"Come to count them?" he said, and put his shoulder to the inner door.

The dark inside was enormous. Light came down from slits under the roof in dusty blades, and in the blades the sacks rose in walls, in terraces, in cliffs — rough hemp, stencilled with the cinder-and-wick in faded black, stacked twenty high on timber pallets and running back into shadow further than the lamps reached. Sparrows moved in the rafters. Grain dust hung so thick in the light that it looked like weather.

"Eighteen thousand sacks," Hobb said, with an odd pride. "Your father bought the lot two harvests back. Every man at the Corn Hall said barley would go to the moon."

"It went to three marks," Mira said. "I passed the slates on the way. Three a sack, and the chalk boy looked embarrassed writing it."

Lys did the sum because it was there to be done. Eighteen thousand sacks at three marks: fifty-four thousand marks. Twenty marks to the crown. Two thousand seven hundred crowns. Against forty-seven thousand two hundred of debt, it was a rounding error with a roof on.

"Two thousand seven hundred," he said.

"If you could sell it. Which you can't, because every house on the river has a granary full of the same." Mira slapped the nearest sack and a small ghost of dust rose off it. "A monument to your father's timing."

Hobb's jaw set. "He was a good trader."

"He was," Lys said, before Mira could answer. They both looked at him. "He was a good trader and he was bad at debt. He bought at the top with borrowed money and then the harvest came in fat, and he couldn't wait for the price to come back because the note came due first. That's not a stupid man. That's an unlucky one who borrowed short against something slow."

Hobb looked at him as if a sack had spoken.

Mira was watching him too, and not the way she had in the hall. More the way a teamster watches a bridge before driving onto it.

"The lease," Lys said. "How many years?"

"Ninety-nine, signed by your great-grandfather," said Hobb. "Eleven left."

So the building wasn't even theirs to sell. The Company owned a roof it rented, a mountain of grain nobody wanted, and a Seat it couldn't sell and was about to lose. He had seen worse balance sheets. He could not immediately remember where.

* * *

The shortest way back from the river to Tallow Street ran up Scale Street, past the lower temple of Sael.

The Temple of the Balance was a plain, square building with a bronze scale-beam over the door, green with age and hung perfectly level. On the steps a thin man in a grey habit, ink to the second knuckle of both hands, was pinning a long sheet to the notice board with more care than Crell had shown a writ. A handful of factors in good coats had drifted over to read it, the way men drift to a fire.

"Lesser Hammer list," Mira said. "Sael's Day."

The ninth. Six days out. Four days before the Court.

Lys stopped.

The factors skimmed the list, and he watched their interest leave them one by one. A road-toll renewal. A lamp-blessing for a dyers' street. Fishing rights in the delta. One man snorted at something near the middle, said a word to his neighbour, and both of them walked away.

Lys stepped up and read what they had snorted at.

> *Lot 7. The Rain-Tithe of the Harrowdales — forfeit by House Oskel — rebid. Rain suspended one season pending tithe.*

He read it a second time.

"Dull," said Mira at his shoulder. "Oskel didn't pay her tithe, so the Harvest-Mother took the rain back. Whoever buys it pays grace up front for a right that earns nothing till autumn. The houses won't touch it."

Rain suspended one season.

The Harrowdales were two days' ride south. Mira knew every village in them. Lys knew, from four nights of the Company's old grain accounts, that they grew something like two sacks in every five that fed Vessant. Spring sowing was already in the ground down there. The growing season was spring and summer. *One season* meant the whole of it.

No blessed rain on the Harrowdales, this year, from now until the autumn.

He saw it the way he used to see things on a screen at two in the morning: not a lot but a supply shock, printed in plain ink on a temple wall, and nobody at the Corn Hall had chalked it into the price. Three marks a sack, because every granary was full and last year was fat. Three marks right up until the day the Harrowdale carts came in half-empty, and then four, and then whatever frightened people would pay. For months. The market wasn't wrong about barley. It just hadn't read the notice board.

And Mourngate held eighteen thousand sacks of it.

His hands were cold. He put them in his coat.

"Brother." His voice sounded odd to him. "A question of auction law."

The thin man turned. He had a narrow, clever face and the patient eyes of someone who had spent years being right in rooms that didn't want him. He took in the blood crusted at Lys's nostril, then Mira, and something in his expression adjusted.

"Master Vey," he said. "Oren Vale. I stand steward here. You'll forgive me — I don't think you've ever asked me a question before. What law?"

"If a guild wins a charter at the Exchange. Is it true that no mortal writ can touch that guild afterwards?"

"Not the guild. The charter holder, and what it holds." Oren's mouth thinned into something drier than a smile. "The winner stands under the god's hand. No mortal writ may seize from it for ninety days. Sael does not like the things he sells being snatched off the counter." He looked at the list, then back at Lys, and the dryness sharpened. "I also stand clerk for the tally-glass, Master Vey. Cinderwick's grace was nine gleams at last reckoning."

"It still is."

"The bid bond is twenty."

"I know."

"And you're asking about Lot Seven." Oren folded his ink-black hands into his sleeves. "May I ask with what?"

"Not yet," Lys said.

He could feel Mira beside him, very still.

"Well." Oren looked at him a moment longer, weighing, the way the bronze beam above the door would weigh. "When you do know, you'll find me here. I'm curious which of us is a fool."

* * *

The strongbox was where he'd left it. The teamsters were on the bench outside, sharing a pipe, and they watched him go in with the flat faces of men who expected to be let down again and had brought tobacco for the wait.

Mira shut the door behind her.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. You've been walking like a man carrying eggs since Scale Street."

He laid it out on the desk with his hands: a bare board, a candle, and a fistful of barley he'd scooped into his pocket at Mourngate without quite meaning to.

"No rain on the Harrowdales this season," he said. "You heard the list. Two sacks in five of the city's grain come out of those valleys. This year they won't. By high summer, everyone in Vessant will know it. By the autumn, barley isn't three marks. I don't know what it is. More. A lot more."

"So sell nothing," Mira said slowly. "Sit on it."

"We don't sell the barley. Not a sack. But sitting on it doesn't save us. Dain's writ comes due in ten days, and the price won't move that fast. We need the stay." He tapped the board. "We bid for the Rain-Tithe."

She laughed. It came out of her short and harsh, like a cough. "With nine gleams."

"With more than nine."

"Where from? The temple sells grace at a hundred and twenty crowns the gleam, and you've got two hundred and twelve crowns and a smith who wants his money. That's one gleam and change. You couldn't post the bond."

"Not from the temple. Not from us." He leaned on the desk. "Mira. Who in the world wants rain on the Harrowdales more than anyone?"

She opened her mouth. Shut it.

"The people who farm it," he said. "Thirty, forty villages that are going to watch their fields go to dust this summer and don't yet know it. Every village has a shrine. Every shrine holds some grace — tithes, prayers, kept promises at harvest. A few gleams here, a few there. None of them can bid at the Exchange. They haven't got a Seat." He heard his own voice quicken and let it. "We have a Seat. For ten more days. So we sell the farmers their own rain. They pledge us their grace now. If we win, they get the rain back this autumn and pay a fixed rain-rent for ten years — low, written down, witnessed. If we lose, the bond comes back and every pledge goes home."

"And if you win and it isn't enough? If the Court sits anyway?"

"The Court can't touch a charter holder for ninety days."

"And on the ninety-first?"

"Then I'll have had ninety days." He met her eyes. "I'll need them."

Mira looked at the barley grains scattered across the board. She pushed one with a fingertip, the way she might test a wheel pin.

"You're asking me to ride two days south, tonight, and go door to door telling farmers a bankrupt drunk wants their shrine money for rain the gods have already taken away."

"Because they know your face and not mine. And because you'll tell them the truth."

"Which is?"

"That it's a gamble. That I've shown you every number I have. And that there isn't anyone else coming."

Then she held out her hand.

He didn't understand, and then he did. He slid the paper across. She read it again, in full this time, lips moving on the long clauses, and folded it in four and put it inside her coat, against her shirt.

"Get it witnessed while I'm gone," she said. "If Brother Oren won't do it, I want it back in my hand when I'm home."

"He'll do it."

"And tell my lads. Not the numbers. That I'm staying." At the door she stopped. "Lysander."

"Yes."

"If this is drink talking, I'll know by the first village. And I'll come back and break your arm."

"That seems fair."

The door closed. A minute later he heard her voice outside, low, and then hooves, going south.

Lys sat down in front of the Charter Stone. Its light had settled back into a thin, steady vein. Two hundred and twelve. Nine gleams. Under lien. Behind his breastbone the Bind pulled gently, like a hand on a sleeve, and the stone's light seemed to pull with it, as though they shared one pulse.

In his old life, when a plan like this failed, a company died, and he flew home and billed for the time.

If this failed, he would not lose a guild. He would sit down on the floor of the Guild Court on the thirteenth of Seedmoon, polite as anything, and die.
