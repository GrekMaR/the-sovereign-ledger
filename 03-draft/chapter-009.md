# Chapter 9: The Sovereign Ledger

The lamp went dim at the three-hundredth step, and Mira stopped walking.

"That's not the draught," she said.

It wasn't. The emberstone in its iron cage still glowed, a coal the size of a hen's egg, but the light it threw had shortened, as if something in the dark ahead was drinking the far end of it. Lys held his own lamp higher. The Deepway ran down in front of them, a throat of red rock worn smooth by thirty years of carts, and the heat came up it like breath from an oven door. His shirt had been wet since the first gate-post. The rock under his palm, when he steadied himself, was warm as a sleeping animal.

"Maruth's rule says walk to the first gate and back," he said. "It doesn't say the Deepway has to like us."

"It says nothing about what lives in it, either." Mira had her knife out, which was pointless and comforting. The scar through her eyebrow shone with sweat. "Hobb's cousin carted emberstone for Dain. Told him there were things down here that ate lamps."

"Ate lamps."

"Ate the light off them. His words."

Something moved at the edge of the glow. Lys did not see it so much as see the light bend around it — a paleness, a drift, the shape of a sheet hung to dry and then forgotten. The lamp flame shrank to a bead. The heat did not change. Only the brightness went, pulled toward the drift in thin, trembling strands.

"Cover it," Lys said.

Mira dropped her cloak over her lamp. He shut the shutter on his. The dark that came down was total, the kind that pressed on the eyeballs, and in it he heard the thing pass: not footsteps, but a soft dry sound like grain poured slowly from a sack.

He opened the other eyes.

It cost what it always cost. A nail went in behind his left eye and stayed there, and the Deepway lit up.

Not with lamp-light. With threads. Since the covenant carts had rolled into the poor-houses and the Stone had tipped him into Warden House, Ledger-sight no longer waited for him to touch a thing; it showed him every witnessed contract in the room, and how hard each one was pulling. Down here the room was the mountain. Gold ran through the rock walls in cables as thick as barge hawsers, braided, old, humming — one vast thread sunk into the stone above and the stone below, taut as a bowstring and not fraying anywhere he could see.

A contract. The Cindermaw itself was a contract. Maruth and the mountain had made terms long before there was a Vessant to sell the lamps to — the mountain gave up its fire, the god kept something in return — and the whole dungeon was the working of that bargain. Theo Marsh had spent sixteen years reading loan covenants for the bones of a business. He had never read one he could stand inside.

The pale thing drifted past him, a hand's width away, feeling for light it could not find. His threads meant nothing to it. It went on up the Deepway toward the surface, grain-pour whisper fading.

"Lys." Mira's hand found his sleeve in the black. "You've gone still. You do that before you bleed."

"Give me a moment."

Among the gold hawsers ran a second thread, thinner and newer, strung from the surface down toward the gate. This one was not gold. It was grey shading to rust, and near its lower end it had gone the red-black of a scab, the colour of a promise halfway to broken. When he looked at it, it seemed to groan, the way a rope groans on a capstan a turn before it parts.

Dain's concession. A thirty-year grant in its last month, and it was straining like something with a stone tied to it.

The nail went deeper. Warmth ran over his lip and he tasted salt and iron.

"Now," Mira said, and pulled the cloak off her lamp before he could argue. The light came back ordinary and yellow. She looked at his face and handed him a rag without comment.

Somewhere above them, the rock gave a long, grinding complaint. A trickle of grit ran down the wall. Then a rattle; then, thirty paces back the way they'd come, a slab the size of a cart-bed slid from the ceiling and settled across half the passage with a sound that went through Lys's boots.

Neither of them spoke until the dust stopped moving.

"Half," Mira said. "We can climb half."

"Then let's go and touch the gate before the mountain renegotiates."

* * *

The first gate of the Cindermaw was not a gate. It was two pillars of black basalt with nothing between them but a hotter dark, and a waist-high post beside them where every load carried up the Deepway paid Maruth's share. A stone bowl on a ledge held the tithe: one emberstone in twenty, left for the god, gone by morning. Into the post's softer wood some factor of House Dain had cut notches in tens and hundreds, season after season, a tally older than Lys's borrowed body.

He put his hand on it anyway, because that was how he'd learned to read, and the red-black thread under his palm jerked like a hooked fish.

The tally for the summer was plain. Eleven hundred loads, notched, the tithe paid on each.

He had other numbers. Since Highsun, when the emberstone hulls' season contracts ran out, Dain's emberstone had gone down the Saltreach the same way everything went down the Saltreach: on Cinderwick barges, with Pell's manifests in a box under the counting-room table. Lys had read those manifests the night the Deep Hammer was posted, looking for something else. Nineteen hundred loads, near enough, carried from the Cindermaw quay to the forges of the lower city since the first of Highsun.

Eight hundred loads with no stone in the bowl. Every summer, if the thread's rot was anything to go by, for years.

Not a clerk's mistake. A cooked tally, notched short by the same hand every season, and a god who had been underpaid long enough for his contract to start tearing.

"Master Vey."

The voice came from beyond the lamplight, courteous as a drawing room. Aurel Dain sat on a fallen block a little way off the path, his own lamp shaded on the stone beside him, his silver-grey hair dark with sweat at the temples. No Crell. No servant. He had taken off his coat and folded it across his knees, lining inward, the way a man folds a coat he means to wear again in company.

"Ser Aurel." Lys took his hand off the post. Not fast. Fast would have told the man something.

"I wondered whether you'd come yourself or send a proxy and find out the hard way that Maruth doesn't take them." Dain's eyes went to the rag at Lys's nose, then to Mira, who had stopped two paces behind him with her knife still out. "Captain Thorne. You can put that away. I'm fifty-six and I've walked down a mountain. If I meant you harm I'd have brought someone younger."

Mira didn't put it away. She lowered it, which Lys recognised as her version of manners.

"You walked alone," Lys said.

"My grandfather walked alone. So did my father. The Deep Hammer has come round four times in a hundred and twenty years, and a Dain has sat on this stone before every one of them." Dain rested his forearms on the folded coat. "I didn't think I should be the one to break the habit."

Lys sat on the far end of the block. The heat from the gate pressed on the side of his face. Up close, Dain smelled of cedar and a long walk; beneath the courtesy he looked tired in a way Lys had not seen on the Exchange floor.

"You'll bid everything," Lys said.

"I'll bid what I have left, which you know to the gleam, because you or your spectacled friend have bought it all by now." A small, dry smile. "The Harvestmoon deliveries broke me three ways. I'll pledge my Seat against the rest. It's the only thing I own that you can't rent me back at a markup."

"I didn't set the price of grain, Ser Aurel. The sky did."

"You set the price of the barges." Dain said it without heat. "Let me tell you what I think, since it's dark and nobody's writing it down. Thirty years my house has kept the ward-lanterns lit on the river wall. In the wet winter, the year the Saltreach froze at the edges, Vantheil's forges went cold and Ruske's went cold and ours did not, because one house held the Deep and one house answered for it. Seven houses on the Concord. Forty Seats. That is not greed, Master Vey. That's a small number of hands on the levers, so that when something goes wrong, somebody can be found and made to put it right."

"And when the hand on the lever is the thing that's wrong?"

Dain's gaze moved — very slightly — to the tally-post, and back.

"Then the other hands correct it," he said. "Quietly. As gentlemen do."

Behind Lys, Mira made a sound in her throat.

"You've got dockers holding shares in a district charter," Dain went on. "Farmers holding rain. Barge-masters, a Syndicate of shadow-dealers, a lower-temple priest with a grudge against the upper temple. Many hands. Tell me who answers when the ward-lanterns go out in the next wet winter. Tell me who a god reaches for."

It was a better question than Lys wanted it to be. He had the answer from his old life — governance, accountability, a board that could sack the chairman — and none of the words existed down here. He looked for the translation and found only the post, and the bowl, and eight hundred loads with no stone in it.

"Me," he said. "For now. I'm bound to the Stone, Ser Aurel. If I go wrong, it kills me. That's a sort of answering."

"It's a sort of answering that dies with you." Dain stood, shook out his coat, and did not put it on. "I liked your father. He was a good trader and bad at debt, and I bought his note at thirty in the hundred because I thought his son would be worse at both. I was wrong about the son. I'd like it noted I'm rarely wrong twice."

He took up his lamp and went on up the Deepway, a slow, upright man climbing out of the heat. Nobody had told him about the slab. Lys found he did not want him to find it in the dark.

"There's a fall at the three-hundredth step," he called. "Keep to the left wall."

The lamp paused. "Thank you, Master Vey."

Then it went on.

Mira waited until it was a spark. "You saw something on that post."

"Yes."

"Something that sinks him."

"Something that could."

"Then you stand on the lip of that mine tomorrow and you say it out loud, in front of everyone, and let the god hear it." Her voice was low and hard. "He had my wages stopped. He had Hobb beaten in his own granary. He sold the city bread he didn't have, and he'd have let you die in your hall with your insides coming loose, and sent flowers."

"And if I say it out loud," Lys said, "I'm telling a god, in front of a crowd, that a mortal has been robbing him for years and he never noticed."

Mira opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Masks don't show embarrassment," Lys said. "I'd rather not find out what they show instead."

He touched the post once more, for the numbers, and let the nail in his skull tell him when to stop.

* * *

The Deep Hammer was held at dawn on Harvestmoon 15, on a shelf of black rock at the Cindermaw's mouth where the heat came out of the mountain in a steady wind and bent the air like water.

No Dawn Stair here, no marble, no gallery of forty Seats. Maruth Below did not climb for anyone. A crowd had come up the mine road regardless — carters, forge-hands, two dozen house factors in good linen already ruined by soot — and they stood well back behind a line of iron stakes that Lys suspected had not been put there for decoration. Oren stood at his elbow with the terms in a leather case, ink on his fingers, the tally-glass from the Temple of the Balance hung round his neck in a pouch. Garrow Finch had come up from Low Wharrow to watch, arms folded, taking up the room of two men. Pell had been left in the counting room with the manifests and strict instructions to sleep. He would not have.

Maruth stood in the mouth of the mine.

Tall, as they all were; taller than the others, or the shelf made him so. His mask was dark horn with no eyeholes, cracked across one cheek, and the crack glowed the colour of the emberstone in Lys's lamp. His robes went down into the dark and did not seem to end. In front of him, on a slab of basalt, lay a hammer of black stone with a haft as long as Lys's arm.

A priestess of Sael — the Weigher's hammer was absent, but not his law — called the lot.

"The concession of the Cindermaw, held of Maruth Below: the right to cut and carry emberstone from the Deep for thirty years, the tithe of the Deep to be paid at the first gate. Bidders who have walked the Deepway may come forward. Bond is twenty gleams."

Two came forward. Vantheil's heir had sent a proxy, and the proxy had been turned back at the road by nothing Lys could see; he stood among the factors now, very pale.

Dain bid first, and he did not waste anyone's time.

"Nine hundred gleams," he said, clear in the hot wind, "and I pledge the Seat of House Dain as surety that the tithe will be paid."

A murmur ran along the iron stakes. Nine hundred was every gleam the house could scrape, borrowed from what remained of the Concord against assets that were mostly promises now. And the Seat — a Seat could not be sold, but it could be forfeit, and a man who pledged one was telling a god he would rather lose his chair than lose the Deep.

Lys could not match nine hundred and keep the Company breathing. He had never meant to.

"Cinderwick bids three hundred gleams," he said, "and offers terms."

Oren stepped forward and opened the leather case. His hands were not quite steady. His voice was.

"By the old law of the Hammer, a bidder may offer terms in place of the whole sum." He read. "The Cinderwick Company pledges three hundred gleams at the fall of the hammer. It pledges a fixed share of the rain-rent of the Harrowdales for the life of the concession. It pledges the carriage of every load from the Cindermaw quay on its own barges, at its own cost, so that the Deep's stone reaches every forge and ward-lantern on the Saltreach. It pledges a tithe on every note of the counting-house that is backed by emberstone. It pledges that those who cut the stone shall hold shares in what they cut, as the Wharrow Brotherhood holds shares in Low Wharrow, and be paid in coin, not in scrip against a master's goodwill."

Garrow, behind the stakes, did not smile. He unfolded his arms.

Oren looked at the last line and then, briefly, at Lys. They had argued over it until the lamp in the temple kitchen burned down.

"And it pledges," Oren read, "to pay the tithe the Cindermaw is actually owed. Counted at the gate by the Deep's own tally, and against the barge manifests of the Saltreach both, so that no load goes up the Deepway that is not paid for twice over in the counting."

That was all. No names. No accusation. Just a sentence that meant nothing to anyone on the shelf who had not read a tally-post in the dark.

The horn mask turned.

Not toward Lys. Toward Dain.

It held there. The crack across its cheek brightened, the way a coal brightens when a door opens somewhere and air comes through. Lys opened the other eyes — only for a breath, all he could afford — and saw the gold hawsers of the mountain running out of the mine mouth and through the shelf beneath their feet, and the concession thread strung between Dain and the dark, red-black from end to end now, and pulling.

Dain looked at the mask, then at Lys. His face did not change. His hands, at his sides, closed.

He understood. So did the god. That was the whole transaction, conducted without a word, in the time it took the priestess to draw breath.

"Does House Dain raise?" she asked.

Dain could have. He had grace left somewhere; a house like his always had one more purse. But raising now meant promising, before a god who had just been told, to pay an honest tithe for thirty years — and an honest tithe was the exact margin his house had been living on. Lys watched him do the sum. He watched him reach the bottom line.

"House Dain does not raise," Dain said.

One heartbeat. Two. Mira's breath, very quiet, beside him.

Three.

Maruth lifted the black hammer and brought it down on the basalt, and the sound did not ring. It went into the rock, and down, and Lys felt it arrive somewhere under his feet long after it had left the air.

Oren fumbled the tally-glass out of its pouch. The disc of smoked crystal clouded, then cleared, and the light inside it wrote what the Charter Stone in Tallow Street was writing at the same moment.

Nineteen thousand two hundred. Eight hundred short of Sovereign House. Lys stared at the figure and felt nothing he could name, which alarmed him more than fear would have.

Behind the stakes, the factors were already moving — not toward Lys, but toward the road, toward the city, toward the counting rooms of houses that had lent Dain grace against a Seat and now held the paper on a man with no concession, no deliveries, and no income beyond his rents. Lys knew that motion. He had watched it in another life, in glass towers, the morning after a covenant breach: creditors running to be first at the door.

Crell had appeared at Dain's elbow from wherever he'd been kept waiting, ledger under his arm. He spoke low. Dain did not answer him. He crossed the shelf instead, unhurried, and stopped in front of Lys with the heat bending the air between them.

"Neatly done," he said. "You could have hanged me with it."

"I could have tried."

"You'd have won." Dain's eyes were grey and very steady. "You'll want to understand why you didn't. I'd think about that, if I were you, before you do it to the next man." He inclined his head, precisely as deep as courtesy required, and not a hair deeper. "The Guild Court sits in three weeks. I expect my creditors will ask it for my Seat. I expect they'll get it."

He walked away down the mine road with Crell a pace behind, and nobody moved aside for him, and nobody stopped him either.

* * *

The crowd went. Oren went, to write the terms into the temple's book before anyone could argue about the wording. Garrow went, stopping only to grip Lys's forearm once, hard, with a hand like a hawser, and say nothing, which from Garrow was a speech. Mira stayed. She sat on an iron stake with her boots dangling and watched him the way she watched a wheel she didn't trust.

Lys walked into the mouth of the mine.

Not far. Ten steps, where the dawn stopped and the heat began. The rock here was scored with thirty years of cart-wheels, and from the dark beyond came the smell of hot iron and old smoke and something mineral he had no word for. Somewhere below, men would go down this road tomorrow with picks and baskets, and the stone they carried up would go into Cinderwick carts, onto Cinderwick barges, into Cinderwick warehouses in Low Wharrow, and be paid for in notes from the Cinderwick counting-house, and the grain the forge-hands bought with those notes would be rain-grown under a Cinderwick tithe.

He counted it the way he'd once counted a client's supply chain on a whiteboard in Canary Wharf: source, carriage, storage, credit, sale. Every link. There was no link left that he did not hold. In his old life there was a word for that, and people went to prison for it, or else got very rich and were photographed at charity dinners.

He opened the other eyes one last time. The gold of Maruth's hawsers ran all around him, into the roof and the floor, and now a new thread ran out of his own chest and down into the dark with them, bright, grey-gold, pending — his. A promise he would have to keep for thirty years. The Oathbrand on his left wrist lay quiet. Korrath, at least, was satisfied with him this morning.

The nail went in. He closed the eyes before it could find the bone.

When he came out, blinking, into the grey light, Veyra was leaning against the rock beside the mouth as though it were a lamp-post on Tallow Street. Smoked-glass spectacles, silver streak in her hair, a fan she wasn't using. He had not seen her in the crowd. He never did.

"Congratulations, Master Vey," she said pleasantly. "You're the cartel now."

Mira, on her stake, went very still.

Lys looked at Veyra. Then back at the mouth of the Cindermaw, where the heat came out in a steady breath, and at the city below, strung along the Saltreach in smoke and slate, every roof of it lit by lamps that would burn his stone.

Dain had asked who answered when the hand on the lever went wrong. The honest reply, this morning, was nobody. The Stone would kill him if the Company failed; it would do nothing at all if the Company succeeded and turned rotten, and rotten was the more likely road. He had watched it happen to better men than Aurel Dain, men who had also believed they were the exception.

"Then I'll need a charter," he said, "that can take it away from me."

Veyra's fan stopped halfway open. "From you?"

"From anyone. Me first." He wiped the last of the blood from his lip and started down the mine road toward the city, and his own hall, and the Stone. "Mira. Find Pell. Tell him to bring clean paper, and a lot of it."
