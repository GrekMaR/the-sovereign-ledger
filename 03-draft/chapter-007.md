# Chapter 7: The Cartel Break

The chalk boy at the Corn Hall wrote the one, rubbed its edge with his thumb as if it might come off, and then wrote the two.

Twelve marks. Bread-grain, per sack, Emberwane the sixth.

Lys felt it before he had finished reading it. The Oathbrand on his left wrist woke like a coal someone had blown on, a ring of heat under the cuff that climbed into the small bones of his hand. He closed the hand. It didn't help.

Around him the Corn Hall did what every market did when a number crossed a line everyone had been watching. It went quiet for the length of a breath, and then very loud. A miller in a floury apron swore at the slate as though it had insulted his mother. Grain dust hung in the high windows, gold in the morning light, and every mote of it had just become more expensive.

Pell was at his elbow with the wax tablet already open. "That's it. That's the number."

"I can read, Pell."

"I know. I only—" The boy swallowed. "Twenty thousand sacks. At four."

"Yes."

"To the poor-houses."

"I was there when I swore it." Lys turned for the doors with his cuff pressed to his ribs. "Find Mira. Mourngate, within the hour. And bring the stock book, not the fair copy."

* * *

Mourngate smelled as it always did: old barley, damp stone, the river breathing wet rope and weed through the loading doors. Hobb had the count ready before anyone asked, chalked on a plank propped against a pillar because his knee would no longer carry him up the ladder to the tally-board.

"Sixteen thousand eight hundred," he said. "Every one I'd swear to. The rest walked off in Seedmoon on a Dain barge, and you took coin for 'em instead of grain."

Five marks a sack. It had seemed a handsome settlement at the time.

Lys looked at the plank and did the sum he had done in his head for a month of nights. Twenty thousand owed. Sixteen thousand eight hundred in hand. Three thousand two hundred short, to be bought at twelve so that it could be sold at four: thirty-eight thousand four hundred marks, better than nineteen hundred crowns, out of the strongbox for the privilege of losing money. Then the barley itself. Every sack he carted to a poor-house at four was a sack he could have sold tomorrow at twelve. Eight marks on each of twenty thousand. Eight thousand crowns, near enough, handed from Cinderwick to the hungry of Vessant by a god who did not do near enough.

In another life he would have called it a contingent liability and buried it in an appendix. Here it lived on his wrist.

Mira came in with the river on her boots and the news already on her face. She didn't bother with greeting.

"Don't do it."

Pell looked up from the stock book. "Don't—?"

"I heard the number in the street. Half of Tallow Street heard it." She jerked her chin at the grey hills of sacking. "That's the war chest. That's the barge leases paid through summer, the Wharrow levees shored, wages for forty dockers who walked off Dain's quays because you told them they'd be paid. You give it away at four and we're a charity with a Seat."

"And if I don't, Korrath takes me."

"Korrath takes your labour. For what, five years? Ten?" She said it flatly, the way Lys imagined she had once told a village headman that the road through his valley was gone. "I've seen men work off oath-debts. It's ugly. It ends. You'd live, and the Company would still have its barley."

"The Company would have a master in the Vault's service," Pell said. His voice cracked on *service*. "Everyone would know. The villages pledged because we keep what we swear. The dockers took their stake because we keep what we swear—"

"The villages pledged because they want rain. People forgive a great deal once their fields are wet."

She was right about the arithmetic. That was the trouble. The frightened part of Lys, the part that had spent sixteen years telling boards to cut the loss-making division and keep the core, had already drawn up her plan and found it sound. Strip the liability. Preserve the asset. He caught himself pricing her: a caravan captain, four months of back wages converted to scrip, a network of thirty-one villages worth more than the barley. A line item who happened to be correct.

He rolled back his cuff instead.

The brand was an angry ring of letters he could not read. When he let the Ledger-sight open—the nail behind the eye, the sharp copper taste at the back of the throat—a thread of grey light ran from it out through the loading doors and up the mountain toward the Vault. Pending. Not yet kept, not yet broken.

"What do we sell, Mira?"

"Barley. Barge space. Warehouse room."

"We sell promises. Rain to thirty-one villages at a fixed rent for ten years. Wages to the Brotherhood. Twenty per cent of the barge profits to the Lantern Syndicate." He let the sight close. His nose was wet; he wiped it with the back of his hand and didn't look at the red. "The first time we break one because it got expensive, every other one gets priced at what it's worth when it's expensive. Which is nothing."

"That's a pretty speech to make over eight thousand crowns."

"It's not a speech. It's the valuation." He pulled the cuff down. "We deliver. All twenty thousand."

Mira held his eye long enough that Hobb found something urgent to do with his chalk. Then she exhaled through her nose.

"Then you'd best find three thousand two hundred sacks," she said, "in a city where everyone who has grain wants you dead."

* * *

The Gilded Concord found him first.

By evening on the seventh, Pell came back from the Corn Hall with his tablet as smooth as he had taken it. No Concord factor would write a Cinderwick order at any price. Merrow's granary clerks put up their shutters when Cinderwick carts turned into the lane. Ruske's carters, who had hauled for Harlan Vey for twenty years, discovered they were fully engaged until Mistmoon. Castellane's chandlers would not even sell them tallow, which was petty, and which Lys admired a little for its thoroughness.

On the ninth came the rumour.

It arrived the way the best rumours did, from three directions at once, never from anyone who had said it first. Cinderwick had missed a payment to the Vault of Vows. The Warden had been seen on Tallow Street with a writ. Korrath would call the loan before Harvestmoon, and everything the boy had bought with it—barges, warehouses, the dockers' wages—would go back up the mountain with him.

It was false in every particular. The Vault's instrument ran to the end of next year and had not missed a day. That did not matter. A rumour did not need to be true; it needed only to be cheaper to believe than to check.

Garrow Finch filled the guildhall doorway that night with his cap in both fists. The Brotherhood's head did not come to Tallow Street often.

"My lads are asking if their stake's worth the ink."

"The loan isn't due until the end of next year," Lys said.

"I know it. You know it. A man with four children and half his wage in your scrip knows what he heard in the tavern last night." Garrow turned the cap a quarter-turn. "I'm not here to pull out. I'm here to tell you there's some who want to. They're asking if Dain'll take them back."

Lys let the sight open, just enough to see the room. Tier II gave him everything witnessed within these walls: the gold filament of the Brotherhood's stake running from Garrow's chest to the Charter Stone, the grey of pending wage-scrip, the Rain-Tithe's steady gold burning somewhere south of his feet. Garrow's thread held. Lys could see that it held.

He could not see how hard it was pulling.

"Tell them to wait ten days," he said. "Tell them that on the eighteenth they can come to the poor-houses in the lower city and count what a guild in default gives away."

"And if they won't wait?"

"Then they'll be the ones who left the week before it paid."

The big man grunted, which might have been a laugh, and ducked back out under the lintel.

Lys sat with the ledger after the hall emptied, the tallow lamp guttering, and drew seven boxes on a scrap of paper. Dain, Oskel, Merrow, Vantheil, Castellane, Ruske, Hale. Sixty thousand sacks sold forward at four marks, due Harvestmoon 1, in a year when the sky over the Harrowdales had forgotten how to rain. Dain alone owed twenty-five thousand. The other six owed thirty-five thousand between them, and every one of them needed a barge and a warehouse he now controlled.

Together, they could starve him of grain and frighten his partners. Separately, each of them would stand alone before the Guild Court on the first of Harvestmoon with a promise it could not keep.

In the old life there had been a name for this. Two prisoners in separate cells, each told that whoever talked first would walk. Seven cells. The Concord's strength was that each house believed the others would hold. It needed only one of them to wonder.

He drew a circle round the box marked *Oskel*.

* * *

House Oskel's townhouse in the upper city had good bones and missing flesh. A rectangle of paler plaster where a tapestry had hung. A sideboard with the marks of candlesticks and no candlesticks. One lamp lit in a receiving room built for twelve.

Lady Benedetta Oskel received him standing, which meant he stood too. She was past fifty, straight-backed, in a gown that had been beautiful three seasons ago.

"You bought my rain," she said.

"I bought Ilvenne's rain. You'd stopped paying for it."

The corner of her mouth tightened. "You're very like your father when you're rude, and not at all like him otherwise. Say what you came to say."

"Your house sold grain forward for Harvestmoon 1 at four marks. You don't have it. The Harrowdales won't grow it this year. The only way to bring it in from outside the delta is by barge down the Saltreach, and I lease sixty of the eighty-four barges on the river. The only dry storage large enough to land it is at Low Wharrow, and I hold that too." He set a folded sheet on the sideboard between the candlestick marks. "The first house to settle with Cinderwick gets barge space and storage at a fair rate and grain delivered to its quay at eight marks. Houses that settle after pay fourteen."

"Fourteen." She didn't touch the paper. "That's extortion."

"That's the price by Harvestmoon, if the sky stays as it is. I'm offering to be wrong in your favour."

"You're asking me to be the first to break."

"I'm asking you to be the first not to drown."

She walked to the window. Below, the city fell away in terraces toward the river, the lower roofs dim with smoke. "They're saying your Vault loan is in default," she said, without turning. "If that's true, your barges are Korrath's by Mistmoon and your offer is worth what I paid for my last tapestry."

He had brought the proof. He laid it out: the Vault's instrument, copied under Sethra's seal, with its date of repayment; the list of barge leases, sixty names of barge-masters and the shrines where each had sworn; the notice Brother Oren would post on the temple door at Scale Street the next morning, announcing the covenant delivery for the eighteenth and inviting any person in Vessant to come and count.

"The covenant costs me eight thousand crowns," he said. "Roughly. I'm buying the shortfall at twelve to sell it at four. A guild in default doesn't do that in public."

The sight was already open; he'd let it come at the door. In this room, too, there were threads. A thin gold one from her to the temple of Ilvenne, old and faded. A cluster of grey, heavy and pendant, that could only be her share of the sixty thousand. He could not see whether it was fraying. He watched her hands instead. She had them folded, and the knuckles were white.

"The Concord has held since before this house had a roof," she said. "If I sign first, Oskel is the house that broke it. That is what my grandchildren will be told."

"If you sign last, Oskel is the house that couldn't deliver. They'll be told that too."

She turned then. Her eyes were very steady.

"Your carts roll on the eighteenth?"

"At dawn."

"Then come back after," she said. "I want to see whether you keep a promise that costs you, Master Vey. Everyone keeps the cheap ones."

* * *

Between moonless nights the Lantern Market was only a vault beneath the Dawn Stair's foundations, with its paper lanterns hanging unlit from the beams like old wasps' nests. Veyra's rooms at the back were not dark. She sat at a trestle with three ledgers open and her smoked-glass spectacles pushed up into her silver-streaked hair.

"You want me to buy three thousand two hundred sacks at the top of the market," she said, "through proxies the Concord won't recognise, so that you can sell them at a loss to paupers."

"Yes."

"And pay me a commission for the privilege."

"Yes."

"Delightful." She wrote something. "Fourteen names. Small lots. It will take four days and you'll pay twelve and a quarter by the end, because someone will notice. Done. What else? You didn't come down here for barley."

"The shadow notes on Harvestmoon delivery. The ones that pay if a Concord house fails to deliver."

She lowered the spectacles to her nose and looked at him over them, which was worse than looking through them. "Nobody buys those. The Concord always delivers."

"Which is why they're cheap."

"A mark on the crown for Dain. Two for the smaller houses." She tapped the ledger. "You're betting against them with news you mean to make true."

"I'm betting on news that's already true. They're short sixty thousand sacks and the sky is dry. All I intend to do is stop a lie that makes it look otherwise." He named the sum: every crown the strongbox could spare after the barley, which was not many.

Veyra wrote that down too, without comment, and then, on a separate line, a larger figure in her own hand.

"Yours?" he said.

"Naturally." She smiled. "I'd hate for you to be right alone."

He didn't ask who had started the rumour about the Vault. She would not lie to him; he was less sure he wanted what she would say instead.

* * *

The carts rolled out of Mourngate at first light on the eighteenth.

Forty of them, begged from every teamster who still took Cinderwick scrip, the sacks stacked in rows of ten under oiled canvas. Hobb stood at the loading doors on his bad knee and called each cart's count aloud, and Pell, beside him, repeated it and wrote it down, and the two voices went on in the grey morning like a litany. Mira drove the lead cart herself.

They had posted the notice, and people had come to count.

The lower city lined Tallow Street and then the long slope down to the river: tanners with stained forearms, washerwomen, dockers in their Brotherhood caps, children barefoot on the cold cobbles. Lys saw factors in good wool at the corners, too, with little books. Let them count. That was the point.

Brother Oren waited at the first poor-house gate with a temple scale borrowed from Scale Street and a face like a man who had not slept. Each sack went on the scale. Each sack was weighed, called, and witnessed under Sael and under Korrath, and the matron of the house, a broad woman with flour to the elbow, paid four marks for it from a purse that the city had filled and had never expected to empty so fast.

"Four marks," Oren said, over and over. "Witnessed."

Lys stood beside the scale and did not open the sight. He didn't need it. The brand on his wrist cooled a degree with every hundred sacks, the way a fever breaks—not all at once, but in long slow steps he could count.

At the third poor-house, a woman with a baby in a sling put her hand on the side of a cart and bowed her head, and her lips moved. Then a man beside her did the same. Then half the line. They were not praying to Lys. They were praying to Korrath, who kept his vows, and to Sael, who weighed them honestly, and to whatever god looks after people who have been told a price and then been charged it.

Something moved up his arm anyway.

It came through the Bind like warmth through a cold hand—not the brand's heat but something deeper and slower, running back along every thread he had ever sworn. He had spent a month treating this covenant as a liability to be survived. A cost line. The thing a sensible man would restructure away. But the ledger he was standing in did not work like that. Here a promise kept at a loss was not a loss. It was the only thing in the city that did not depreciate. Barley, barges, warehouses were worth what someone would pay tomorrow. This was worth what everyone would believe of him for ten years.

He had it backwards. He had always had it backwards.

The last cart emptied at the river poor-house an hour before dusk. Twenty thousand sacks. Oren called the final weight in a voice gone hoarse, and the grey thread from Lys's wrist to the Vault turned, without any fuss at all, to gold.

Pell met them halfway up Tallow Street, running, ink on his chin.

"The Stone," he said. "Master Vey. The Stone's changed."

* * *

He tested it the next morning in the Corn Hall, because a capability he had not used in anger was only a rumour about himself.

The slate read twelve and a half. The Concord's factors stood in their usual knot beneath it, and Lys let the sight open.

The room filled with threads as it had since Greenmoon—gold, grey, a few red-black snarls where some small merchant was going under. But now the grey threads had *texture*. They stretched. He could see load in them the way you could see it in wet rope on a quay: the fibres drawn tight, the lay beginning to open, the fine hairs standing out where strands had already parted. It was not a colour. It was closer to a sound, a thin whine behind the headache, rising where the strain was worst.

The forward contracts ran from the factors toward the upper city in seven heavy bundles.

Vantheil's, Castellane's and Ruske's were taut but whole. Oskel's hummed. Merrow's and Hale's were thin, the strands going pale and wiry, the whine behind them high and sharp. And Dain's—twenty-five thousand sacks of it—was not grey at all any longer. It had gone red-black along half its length, and in places it was held together by a few bright filaments that trembled whenever a factor shifted his weight.

Blood ran warm over his lip. He closed the sight before the floor tilted and pressed Pell's handkerchief to his nose.

"Master Vey?"

"Letters," Lys said thickly. "Merrow and Hale. Same terms I gave Oskel, at fourteen. Tell them there may not be a first house for long."

He went up to the Oskel townhouse that afternoon. Lady Benedetta had watched the carts from the Temple steps; she said so before he'd finished bowing. She signed at Scale Street the next morning with Oren's hand on the witness-scroll and her chin high, and when it was done she looked at the gold thread only Lys could see as though she, too, could feel it settle.

"Eight marks," she said.

"Eight marks."

"I will be told this was the day Oskel betrayed the Concord."

"You'll be told it was the day Oskel delivered."

House Merrow signed on the twenty-first at fourteen, and complained about it in writing. Hale signed the day after, without complaint, which Lys found more worrying. Vantheil, Castellane and Ruske sent no answer at all, and their factors stopped standing with Dain's under the slate.

On the evening of the twenty-second the Stone moved again.

Garrow's dockers stopped asking whether their stake was worth the ink. No one in the Corn Hall mentioned the Vault any longer. And House Dain, which had owed twenty-five thousand sacks as one voice among seven, now owed them alone.

* * *

Aurel Dain came to Tallow Street on the twenty-fourth, alone and on foot.

Lys had never seen him without a carriage or a factor. He walked up the street in grey wool and good boots, nodding to the chandler's widow on her step as though they were acquainted, and stopped at the guildhall door where Crell had once nailed a writ.

"May I come in, Master Vey?"

"You may."

Dain looked at the Charter Stone for some time. Then at the ledgers on the long table, at Pell bent over a page with his pen not moving, at the tallow-stained beams his own bankers had once been glad to be rid of.

"You kept the covenant," he said. "I wouldn't have."

"I know."

"It was well done. I mean that." He took off his gloves, finger by finger. "The Concord has held longer than either of us has been alive. Not because we were fond of one another. Because every house knew what happened to a house that stood alone. You've shown them that isn't so. You've shown them it can be profitable to break."

"It was profitable to deliver. That isn't the same."

"Isn't it?" Dain's voice did not rise; it never did. "You've taught every house in Vessant that the Concord can be broken. Do you imagine you'll be exempt from the lesson?"

Lys didn't answer. He let the sight open instead, just a crack, and looked at the man. The red-black thread ran out of Dain's chest toward the upper city, twenty-five thousand sacks of it, a few bright strands still holding. Nothing about his face said he could feel it.

Dain drew his gloves back on, bowed with perfect courtesy, and walked out into Tallow Street. Lys watched him go until the dusk took him.

"Master Vey?" Pell had his pen raised. "What do I write?"

Lys closed the sight. His wrist was cool now, for the first time in weeks. That did not feel like safety.

"Write that the Concord is broken," he said. "Then start a new page. Head it *Cinderwick*." He sat down across from the boy and pulled the lamp closer. "And list every way someone could do to us what we've just done to them."

Pell dipped the pen. It hovered over the empty page for a long time before he wrote the first line, and Lys, watching it, had no answer ready for how long the list would grow.
