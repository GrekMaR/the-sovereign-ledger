# Chapter 4: The Lantern Market

The stair had no rail, and its steps had been cut for smaller feet than his.

Lys went down sideways, one palm flat on stone that sweated like a cellar wall, and counted. Forty steps. Fifty. Above him the Temple of the Balance slept behind barred doors; on auction mornings a stair of white stone climbed out of it into the sky. This stair went the other way, into the foundations that held the white one up. It smelled of tallow smoke, river damp, and money.

"You're counting," Mira said behind him.

"Sixty-one."

"Why?"

"In case I need to leave faster than I came in."

She grunted. Her boots were steadier on the narrow treads than his. She wore a knife tonight she had never worn to the guildhall. He had not asked.

At the ninetieth step the stair turned, and the dark gave way.

The vault beyond was older than the temple, a forest of squat pillars holding up a ceiling he couldn't see. Between the pillars ran cords, and from the cords hung paper lanterns by the hundred: saffron, oxblood, a sickly river-green, turning on the warm air so the whole market seemed lit from inside a bruise. Beneath them stood trestles, ledgers, and brokers in good coats and bad ones.

Nobody shouted. The Corn Hall roared; this place murmured, the collective hush of two hundred people who each wanted the other hundred and ninety-nine not to hear.

"—Merrow to take the Lesser Hammer lot, three to one—"

"—a mark a sack over, if barley closes above five on Highsun first—"

"—half a gleam and not a bit more, that lot's cursed—"

Shadow notes. Promises on things that had not happened yet: which house would take which lot, what grain would fetch in three months, whether a barge would reach the quay. The part of him that had been Theo Marsh supplied its own words. Forwards. Options. A book on the auction. An exchange beneath the Exchange, where the gods did not come down to watch.

A broker in a moth-eaten coat fell into step beside him and pressed a slip into his hand. "Master Vey. The rain man. Sixty crowns, coin tonight, for your first season's rain-rent from the Harrowdales. Sixty, and you sleep easy."

Lys closed his fingers on the slip and let the Bind open a crack. Nothing. No grey, no gold. No god had watched this promise made; it was worth exactly what the man in the moth-eaten coat was worth, which, judging by the coat, was not sixty crowns.

He handed it back. "Ask me again when it rains."

"Everyone here knows your face," Mira said.

"It's the price of admission. They let us in because they want to read us."

"And what do we get?"

"We read them back."

* * *

The child who led them up the last flight wore a pin shaped like a lantern, the same pin, perhaps the same child, that had delivered Veyra's note to Tallow Street. The room at the top was a gallery cut into the vault wall, open on one side, so its occupant could look down the market like a clerk down a column.

Veyra sat at a table covered in ink, wax, and three sets of scales. Her dark hair was shot with silver and pinned with two steel pens. Smoked-glass spectacles hid her eyes, which he suspected was their purpose.

"Master Vey." Her voice had a smile in it that did not reach anywhere he could see. "You came. And you brought your captain, which means you expect to be cheated or robbed."

"Both," said Mira.

"Sensible. You bought the Harrowdales' rain for a hundred and sixty-four gleams, in the one season it won't fall. The valleys go dry, the harvest fails, and your father's barley stops being a joke. Sixteen thousand eight hundred sacks, I hear, after your careless neighbours." She turned a pen in her fingers. "Everyone in this vault is paying me to learn one thing. What will you do with it?"

"Everyone in this vault can guess."

"They guess you'll sit on the barley until it fetches nine, then sell and pay House Dain. Dull, but it might work." The spectacles tilted. "I don't think you're dull."

"You sent me a note," Lys said. "Sixty thousand sacks sold forward by the Concord, for Harvestmoon first, at four marks. What did that cost you to learn?"

"Less than you'd guess. More than you'll ever pay."

"Then why give it away?"

"I didn't give it. I invested it." She set the pen down. "Here's a small test, since we're testing. Seven houses owe sixty thousand sacks at four marks. If barley stands at nine on the morning they must deliver, what have they lost?"

"Five marks a sack. Three hundred thousand marks." He did not reach for a slate. "Fifteen thousand crowns. More, once every grain-seller in the delta learns they have to buy."

Veyra was quiet for a moment.

"Lysander Vey," she said, "couldn't count his fingers sober."

Somewhere in the smudged ledger of this body's memory there was a picture of her: Harlan's table, candles, a boy of sixteen too drunk to follow the talk, a woman in dark spectacles watching him the way a buyer watches a lame horse.

"I've stopped drinking," he said. "It freed up a great many fingers."

"So I hear. You also stopped flinching. Grief does strange things to men. I've never seen it teach arithmetic."

Lys kept his hands still on his belt and gave Veyra nothing. She seemed to file the nothing for later, which frightened him more than a question would have.

"You're thinking about the barley," she said. "Stop. It's the smallest thing you own."

"Then tell me what the biggest is."

"No. You tell me. That's what I asked you here to find out."

He looked down at the market. The Concord had sold grain it did not have, expecting to buy it cheap. It would not be cheap. So the houses would do what any trader short of stock did: find it elsewhere. Not in the Harrowdales, which would be dust by Highsun. Outside the delta, upriver, from fields with their own rain.

And grain from outside the delta came one way.

"The Saltreach," he said.

Veyra said nothing, which was its own kind of yes.

"Nobody moves sixty thousand sacks by cart. It comes down the river by barge or it doesn't come." He felt the old pleasure, the one that had kept Theo at his desk until the cleaners came. Don't fight over the goods. Own the road the goods have to travel. The middle of the chain. "How many barges work the Saltreach?"

"Eighty-four licensed hulls."

"How many are idle?"

"Sixty," Veyra said. "The rest carry salt, timber, and emberstone on season contracts. The sixty sit on their ropes and rot. Nobody wants to move barley. The masters would kiss your boots for two crowns a week."

"Then I'll take all sixty. Six months."

Mira turned and looked at him.

"Sixty hulls," Lys said, mostly to himself. "Two crowns a week, twenty-six weeks. Three thousand one hundred and twenty crowns."

"And you have," Veyra said, "what? Three hundred?"

"Enough for a week's rent on a dozen."

"You want," Mira said, very evenly, "to rent sixty empty boats, with money you haven't got, to carry grain you don't own, for people who'd like to see you dead."

"Yes."

"Say it slower. It still sounds mad."

"When the Concord comes to the river in Emberwane, looking for hulls, every idle barge on the Saltreach will be wearing our cord. They'll hire from us, at our price, or they'll float their grain home on their good names." He met her eyes. "I'm not selling barley. I'm selling the only way into the city."

Mira's scarred brow drew down. "And the money?"

Both of them looked at Veyra.

"Shadow notes," Veyra said, as if it were obvious. "I sell promises on your barge income to the good people below. Some will believe in you; more will want to bet against the Concord. The coin comes up to me. I pay your masters every week, on the nail, so they never learn to doubt you. In return, the Syndicate takes a fifth of whatever those barges earn above their rent. Twenty in the hundred." She lifted one finger. "And one favour. To be named later."

"No," said Mira.

"Captain—"

"No. You never sign a blank line. My father signed one with a salt-factor and spent a year carrying salt for gratitude." She turned on Lys. "You've spent a month telling me to read everything. Read that."

He had read it. Theo had seen a hundred term sheets with a line like that on the last page, and it was always the most expensive line in the document. A fifth of the profit was a fair price for capital no one else would lend him. The favour was the part he couldn't price, and she had put it there because he couldn't price it.

He also had until Emberwane the ninth. After that, Dain's note stood, the Guild Court sat, and the Bind in his chest came undone like a knot in wet rope.

"Name it," he said to Veyra.

"If I named it, it would be a price, not a favour."

"Then bound it. Nothing that breaks a witnessed contract. Nothing that costs one of my people their life or a village its pledge. Nothing that puts my Seat in anyone's hand but mine."

"Agreed. I never ask for what can't be given. It's bad business."

"And I want it said here, in front of her."

"It's said." Veyra held out her hand across the ink and the scales.

He took it. He let the Bind open, and there was nothing, no thread at all, because no god had been asked to watch. He was trusting a name. He, who trusted numbers because numbers could not change their minds, had just bet his life on a woman whose eyes he could not see.

"Eyes open," he said.

"Mine always are," said Veyra. "It's only that you can't tell."

On the stair up, Mira did not speak until the fortieth step.

"If she calls it in and it's rotten," she said, "I'll be the one who tells you so."

"I know. That's why I brought you."

* * *

They began on the morning of Seedmoon thirtieth, and they began on foot.

Hobb knew the barge-masters because every one had loaded Cinderwick grain at Mourngate in the last twenty years, and he had shouted at all of them. He limped the quays with his knee strapped, and where he stopped, men put down their rope. Mira's teamsters drank in the river taverns and knew whose hull leaked and whose master owed whom. Pell carried the ledger, ruled in two columns the way Lys had taught him, and wrote each lease twice, once where the money went out and once where the hull came in.

The leases were witnessed at Oda's shrines, one at the head of each quay: a niche in the river wall, a weathered figure with a road worn smooth in its open hands, a bowl of river water and a hank of blue cord. The shrine-keeper tied a knot for every lease and spoke the god's name over it; the master pressed a wet thumb to the knot, Lys pressed his, and through the skin he saw the grey thread spring between them, pending.

Two crowns a week. Forty marks in silver, counted out on the shrine step. The first week's coin came from the Tallow Street strongbox; the second from the Lantern Market, carried up at dawn in a plain satchel by the child with the pin, and it was correct to the bit. Lys counted it twice anyway.

On Greenmoon first the Dawn Stair rose out of the Temple of the Balance at first light, and the Concord's silks climbed it for the Dawn Auction. Lys watched them from the grain quay with his thumb wet from a shrine bowl. He signed the twenty-ninth, thirtieth, and thirty-first barges while House Dain bought something in the sky, and when the silks came down at noon, none of them looked toward the river.

That afternoon a rider came in from the south with the dust of the Harrowdales caked in the creases of his coat. Seven days without rain. The spring sowing had come up thin and was yellowing at the tips. In the pledged villages, people had started looking at the sky, then at the road to Vessant.

That evening the Corn Hall chalked barley at four marks. Not a leap. A creep.

By the second of Greenmoon they had fifty-nine.

The sixtieth was the *Patient Heron*, a broad old hull with a patched sail and a master named Hesk who sat on her stern with a pipe and did not get up.

"Cinderwick," Hesk said, when Hobb had made the introduction. The way a man names an old fever. "I carried your father's grain three winters back. Two hundred sacks to the upper wharves. Eleven crowns agreed at Oda's bowl. He paid me four."

"He's dead," Lys said.

"I heard. Didn't bring me the other seven."

Mira opened her mouth. Lys lifted a hand and she closed it again.

"Have you the tally?"

Hesk went below and came back with a square of oiled leather and a knot of faded blue cord inside it. Lys took it.

Red-black.

It ran out of the cord and into his hand like a splinter of cold, and behind his left eye the nail went in. A broken thread. His father's thread, which made it his, because the Bind did not care who had died. He could see where it frayed: the four crowns paid, bright as a scab; the seven owed, rotten.

He had been treating the Charter Stone like a balance sheet. Grace in, holdings in, a number out. But the Stone counted kept contracts, too, and this was the other side of that sum. It wasn't a tally of what the guild owned. It was a record of whose word held. Every master on this river had watched Cinderwick's word fail. Hesk was only the one honest enough to say so.

Seven crowns. Three and a half weeks of the *Patient Heron*, and near all the coin he had that wasn't promised elsewhere.

"Pell," he said. "Seven crowns. From my purse, not the Company's. Write it that way."

Pell counted it out on the stern board. A hundred and forty silver marks, in stacks of twenty. Hesk did not touch them.

"This isn't a lease," Lys said. "This is what you were owed. Take it and tell me to go to the devil, if you like."

"Why?"

Because I need your hull, Lys thought. Because I'm frightened and you're a line item and I can't close the book without you. That was true. It wasn't the whole of it. "Because it's owed," he said, "and I've got his name."

The thread in his hand shivered. Red-black bled to grey, grey to a thin clean gold, like a wick taking the flame.

Hesk looked at the money, then for a long time at Lys.

"Six months," he said finally. "Two crowns. And I want it on the nail, every week, or I'll moor her in the middle of the river and let her sink."

"On the nail," Lys said.

They walked to the shrine at the head of the quay. The keeper tied the sixtieth knot. Hesk wet his thumb and pressed it to the cord, and Lys pressed his, and the grey thread sprang between them—

—and did not stop at them.

Something in his chest turned over, like a heavy page. The shrine niche lit. Not with lamplight: with threads. Dozens, grey and gold, hanging from the god's worn figure like the cords in the Lantern Market: a master's promise to his crew, a widow's lease on a mooring. Threads ran out along the quay to the moored hulls, sixty of them grey and leading back to his own breastbone, and between two bollards a single red-black one, fraying, that ran from a boy with a coil of rope to a man who would not meet his eye.

He wasn't touching any of it. He was seeing all of it.

His nose ran warm. He put his sleeve to it and it came away dark.

"Master?" Pell had his elbow. "Master Vey, you're bleeding."

"I know." He shut his eyes, and the threads were still there, printed on the inside of his lids, until he pushed the Bind down like a lid on a pot. When he opened them, the quay was only a quay. Hesk was staring at him. Mira had a hand on her knife.

"Home," Lys said. "I want to see the Stone."

The Charter Stone stood in the back of the guildhall, waist-high and grey, smelling of the tallow soaked into every board of Tallow Street. Its vein was brighter than he had ever seen it. When he laid his palm on its face, the light moved under his hand like a fish.

Pell read it aloud twice, then sat down on the floor.

"Chartered House," the boy said. "We can bid on districts."

"With twenty-five gleams," Mira said. "Which is to say, we can watch." But she was looking at the number, and her mouth had done something it didn't usually do.

Lys wiped his nose and thought about a thread that had turned from red-black to gold for seven crowns, and how close he had come to not paying them.

* * *

On the third of Greenmoon he took Pell to the Corn Hall to learn what his eyes could do now.

The hall roared as it always did: grain dust hanging gold in the high windows, slates chalked and wiped and chalked again, millers and bakers' agents shouting across the pits. Barley stood at four. Bread-wheat had ticked up a quarter-mark on the rumour of a dry week. Lys stood at the rail and let the Bind open a finger's width.

The hall filled with light.

Not all of it; most of this floor ran on handshakes and slate. But here and there, like knots in a net, men carried promises some god had watched: a miller's grey contract for flour, a baker's gold one kept and closing, a factor's lease on a granary running out through the east doors. And over by the far pit, three men in dark coats with House Dain's seal stamped on their ledger straps, trailing grey threads by the score.

Purchases. Pending. Every one ran out of the hall and away upriver, too far to follow. Grain bought outside the delta, just as he had guessed, and bought quietly.

A heavy man in a salt-stained coat was leaning in to the nearest of them.

"Six hulls," Lys heard him say. "Free by Highsun. I'll carry for House Dain at a fair rate, and I'll carry first."

From the salt-stained man ran three gold threads, the season's salt contract, kept and humming. And six more, grey-gold, that ran across the hall and ended in the middle of Lys's own chest.

Six of his sixty. Leased to Cinderwick, witnessed at Oda's bowl, paid on the nail, and being sold a second time to House Dain for a fee.

"Master," Pell whispered, "your nose."

"Not yet." He waited until the Dain factor moved on, then let the Bind shut, took the handkerchief Pell offered, and walked around the pit to where the salt-stained man was lighting a pipe.

"The *Tern*, isn't it? And the *Sister Moll*, and four others."

The man's face settled on caution. "Master Vey."

"You've six hulls leased to me until the end of Mistmoon, witnessed before Oda. I heard you offering them to House Dain." Lys kept his voice low and friendly. "I'm sure I misheard. If a witnessed lease breaks, it isn't me who comes asking why. It's the god you swore in front of."

The pipe had gone out. The man did not relight it. "You misheard."

"I thought so. Good day."

Walking away, Pell said, "How did you know they were ours? You never saw his tallies."

"I saw it."

"Saw what?"

Lys folded the bloody handkerchief and put it in his pocket. "Who's lying about what they can carry."

* * *

On the morning of the fourth, Crell came to the river.

Lys saw him from the top of the grain quay, where he had sat since dawn on a bollard with a heel of bread. Crell walked neat and unhurried, ledger under his arm, two of Dain's men behind him. He went to the first barge and spoke to the master. The master shook his head and pointed at the blue cord knotted to his mooring post. Crell made a note.

The second barge. The same.

The third master didn't even stand up; he just lifted the cord in two fingers.

By the eighth, Crell had stopped making notes. By the fifteenth, he walked less neatly. At the end of the quay he stood a long while, looking downriver at hull after hull wearing Cinderwick's cord, the *Patient Heron* among them with Hesk on her stern, pipe lit.

Then he turned and looked up the bank, straight at Lys.

He climbed the quay steps without hurrying. His men stayed below.

"Master Vey."

"Master Crell. Early for a stroll."

"Sixty hulls," Crell said. "Six months. You have no grain to carry."

"Not yet."

It was a small thing to watch. Crell's expression did not change. He only stood very still, and Lys could almost see the column adding itself behind those eyes, the way figures shift when one wrong number at the top is corrected: the forward contracts, the upriver purchases, the Harvestmoon deadline, the river, and at the one narrow point where all of it had to pass, a sober boy on a bollard eating bread.

"House Dain," Crell said, "will pay what it must."

"I'm counting on it."

"You should count on more than that." Crell tucked the ledger tighter under his arm. "A man who stands in the middle of a road, Master Vey, is very easy to find."

He went back down the steps. Lys watched him go and did not feel as pleased as he had expected. Owning the only road meant everyone knew where to find you.

A small shadow fell across his boots.

The child with the lantern pin held out a folded paper sealed with a blot of plain black wax. He had not heard her come.

He broke it open. Three lines, in a clerk's hand.

*Madam calls in one favour early.*

*Korrath's Vault is going to make you an offer.*

*Accept it.*

No signature.

He read it three times. The Vault of Vows lent against oaths, and the Oathkeeper did not lend to boys with twenty-five gleams out of charity. Veyra had known of the offer before Korrath's own priestess had said a word, and had spent her favour to make him take it before he had seen the terms.

A fifth of the barges' profit. That, he could price.

He looked up to ask the child what her mistress knew, and what it had cost her, and who else in the Lantern Market had already bought a note on the answer. But the quay was empty, and the river went on carrying nothing, and the only thing left in his hand was a blank line with a god's name on it.
