# Chapter 3: The Price of Silence

Pell's knuckles came fast and uneven on the door, the way a boy knocks when he has run the length of Tallow Street and is still running inside.

"Master Vey. Mourngate. Somebody's been in Mourngate."

Lys was on his feet before he was awake. The candle had drowned in its own tallow some time after midnight, and the ledger he'd fallen asleep across had printed a column of figures on his cheek. He found his boots by stepping on them.

"Hobb?"

"Alive." Pell's voice cracked on the word. "They hit him. He says it's nothing. It isn't nothing, his face is all—" He made a shape with one hand that meant *wrong*.

They went down to the river at a pace between walking and running, through the grey hour before the tanneries woke, when the lower city smelled only of the Saltreach: mud, wet rope, the sweet rot of reed. Mourngate squatted on the bank like a stone ox. Its river doors stood wide. They should not have been wide.

Hobb sat on an upturned bushel measure by the threshold, a wet rag pressed to one eye and his bad leg stuck out straight in front of him. Two warehousemen hovered, useless with relief.

"Don't fuss," Hobb said, before Lys could. "My wife hit harder, and she's been dead nine years."

"How many?"

"Six. Maybe eight. Came off the water quiet as you like and pried the river door. I heard the bar go." He took the rag away. The lid beneath was shut, purple and shining like a plum. "I'd have had one of them, if not for the knee."

"You'd have been killed for one of them." Lys crouched and looked at the eye, then at the hands. Split knuckles. Hobb had hit somebody. "What did they take?"

Hobb's jaw worked. "Come and see."

Inside, the barley stood in long terraced stacks under the roof-beams, Harlan Vey's eighteen thousand sacks, the air so thick with grain dust it tasted of stale bread. Near the river door a gap had been opened in the rows like a pulled tooth. Lys walked it. The missing stacks had left pale squares on the flagstones where the sacks had kept the stone clean, and he counted squares and multiplied by the height of the stacks either side.

"Twelve hundred," Pell said behind him, already there. "I make it twelve hundred."

"So do I."

The other smell reached him a moment later. Lamp oil, rank and fishy, the cheap kind rendered down on the mudflats. It had been poured over the stack nearest the door; the burlap had gone dark with it and the floor was slick. A tinderbox lay open in the puddle, flint still inside.

"They were going to fire it," Lys said.

"They were going to," Hobb agreed, limping up behind them. "Then I came down the stair making a noise like a bull, and they had what they'd come for and didn't fancy a second helping."

Lys stood in the reek and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was what his hands did when the rest of him wanted to shake. Twelve hundred sacks at three marks: three thousand six hundred marks. A hundred and eighty crowns. Nearly everything the guild held in coin, and nothing at all to a house like Dain. Nobody stole barley in a glut. You didn't send eight men off the water in the dark for something any factor could buy at the Corn Hall with a nod.

You sent them to show you could. The oil was the letter. The theft was only the envelope.

"Cut the soaked sacks off and rebag what's underneath," he told the warehousemen. "Sand the floor. Nobody strikes a light in here until it's dry." He turned to Pell. "Run to the Temple. Ask Brother Oren to come and see this with his own eyes, and to bring whatever he writes on."

"You want a witness."

"I want it on paper before anyone tells me it didn't happen."

By the time he got back to Tallow Street, the Charter Stone had noticed. The vein of light in the grey block pulsed once, the way a bad tooth does, and settled.

Fourteen points gone overnight. Sael kept a sharper tally than the Guild Court ever would.

* * *

The rumour reached the Corn Hall before the stolen barley reached wherever it was going.

Pell brought it back on the eleventh with the morning prices, chalk dust on his sleeves. Barley unchanged at three. And a new thing, passed between the factors at the slate boards the way men pass a flask: Cinderwick's rain-shares were paper. The pledges would never be honoured; thirty-one villages had been gulled by a drunk. Lysander Vey had been seen in a dockside tavern the night of the robbery, legless, singing.

"I was asleep on the ledger," Lys said.

"I know. I was asleep on the floor. I told them." Pell scuffed at the boards. "Nobody asked me twice."

By the twelfth it was on the roads. The first rider came in at noon, a farmer's son on a mule too old for the distance, a cracked wax pledge-token on a cord round his neck. His village wanted its grace back. Eleven gleams. He had been told not to come home without it.

The second arrived at dusk. Three more came on the thirteenth, one of them an elder too stiff to dismount without help, who stood in the guildhall with his hat in both hands and asked, very politely, whether it was true.

Lys knew the shape of this. He had watched it once on a screen in the other life: a queue of people in raincoats outside a bank, each of them entirely rational. It hadn't mattered whether the bank was sound, only whether each person in the queue believed the others did. Trust was a debt, and the creditors could call it on demand.

Except here there was nothing to hand back. The grace had gone up the Dawn Stair and into the Harvest-Mother's keeping. Temples turned coin into grace, never the reverse, and no one on earth could unspend a bid.

That night he sat with the list of thirty-one villages and began, without quite deciding to, to sort it. Biggest pledges at the top. Then the villages nearest the rumour, then the ones whose defection would drag others after them. Exposure, weighted. Contagion risk—

"What are you doing?"

Mira stood in the doorway of the counting-room, still in her riding coat. She had spent two days on the south road, meeting riders before they reached the city.

"Working out who matters most," he said.

She came round and read the list over his shoulder. The scar through her eyebrow didn't move. She put one finger on the last line.

"Four gleams," she said.

"It's the smallest pledge."

"It's forty-two people and a hill. The headwoman sold her dead husband's plough-ox to make four gleams. I sat in her kitchen and drank her beer." Mira's finger didn't move. "I swore on my own name in that kitchen, Vey. Not yours. Mine. You put her at the bottom of a list, you've put me there with her."

He looked at the column and saw for a moment what she saw: not exposure but a woman and an ox. It was not a comfortable thing to see at midnight.

"I can't give any of them their grace back," he said. "It's spent. It's the charter."

"I know that. They don't. And they won't take your word for it, because your word's the thing the rumour's about." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger and dropped them on the ledger. "So what have you got that isn't your word?"

He didn't answer. But he began, then, to think about it the right way round.

* * *

On the thirteenth of Seedmoon — the morning the Guild Court should have heard the foreclosure, and his name should have come off the Stone with his life likely following it — Crell came instead with an offer.

He came alone, neat as a folded letter, ledger under his arm. He took in the chipped benches, the tallow-dark beams, and Mira, who had not been asked to leave and did not offer. He declined a chair.

"House Dain is distressed to hear of your misfortune at Mourngate," he said. "Theft. Very nearly arson. And now this unpleasantness on the roads. A young guild can be finished by talk faster than by debt."

"We'd noticed."

"Ser Aurel would like to help." Crell opened the ledger, not to read from it, but so that Lys could see the figures existed. "House Dain will buy the whole of your barley at Mourngate for five marks the sack. Two above the Corn Hall. House Dain will make good your losses. And the talk will stop. Talk does stop, Master Vey, once the people who started it find something better to discuss."

"And in return."

"The Rain-Tithe of the Harrowdales, transferred to House Dain under Sael's witness. Ser Aurel knows you paid a hundred and sixty-four gleams. He wouldn't see you out of pocket."

Lys did the sum because he couldn't not. Sixteen thousand eight hundred sacks at five marks: eighty-four thousand marks. Four thousand two hundred crowns. Real money. Nowhere near forty-one thousand. The note would still sit in Dain's strongroom, the stay would run out on Emberwane 9, and on the tenth Crell would be back on the step with his hammer and nails — and Cinderwick would own nothing a god had noticed and nothing a village would ever trust again.

It was a fine offer, as nooses went. It paid him to tie the knot himself.

"No," he said.

Crell's face did not change. "You might take a day."

"I don't need one. The answer's the same at dusk as at dawn."

"Everything is for sale, Master Vey. That is rather the point of the Exchange."

"Then it isn't for sale to you."

Behind him Mira shifted her weight against the wall. He could feel her not speaking.

Crell closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom. "Your father held out for a better price too," he said. "On barley, as I recall." At the threshold he looked back.

"Silence has a price, Master Vey. So does noise."

The door swung shut behind him. The vein in the Charter Stone flickered in the draught, or seemed to.

"He threatened you," Mira said.

"He priced me." Lys found his hands flat on the table, pressing. "A threat means someone's angry. A price means they've done the sum. And he told me what he's buying. Quiet. A whisper in the Corn Hall, a barge in the dark, a debt note bought at thirty in the hundred in a back office. It all works because nobody reads the books but them."

"So?"

"So we open ours." He looked at the Stone. "In public. All of it."

Mira stared at him. "You want to tell the whole of Vessant how broke we are."

"They already think they know. I want them to know exactly." He was already reaching for fresh paper. You could not out-whisper a whisper. You could only make the truth so complete, and so dull, that there was nothing left to whisper about.

* * *

It took six men, two levers and a set of greased rollers to shift the Charter Stone from the guildhall floor, and Lys felt every inch in his teeth. The Bind did not like the Stone being moved. When he took his palm off the grey block the world went faintly sick at the edges, so he walked beside the cart the whole length of Tallow Street and up the climb to Scale Street with one hand resting on it, like a man steadying a coffin.

"You look like a mourner at your own funeral," Brother Oren observed from the Temple steps, ink to the second knuckle.

"If it goes badly, you can read the service."

"I'd charge you for it." But he came down with the temple's witness-seal on its chain and took his place beside the cart.

It was the fourteenth, and the forecourt of the Temple of the Balance was fuller than Lys had dared hope. Dockers on their dinner hour, chewing. Three factors from the Corn Hall in good wool, come to laugh. Hobb on a stool at the front, his eye gone to a bruised yellow, placed there by Mira as an exhibit. And seven riders from the Harrowdales, dust-grey, in a row by the fountain.

Oren spoke the witnessing — short, dry, old words about the Weigher's scales that do not lie — and nodded to Pell.

Pell climbed onto an empty crate with the ledger in both arms. His voice cracked on the first line. He swallowed and began again.

"Owed to House Dain on a note of hand, forty-one thousand crowns. Owed to small creditors, six thousand two hundred crowns." A murmur ran along the forecourt, more surprise than scorn. Nobody read these numbers aloud. "Barley at Mourngate, sixteen thousand eight hundred sacks, at the Corn Hall price of three marks. Twelve hundred sacks stolen on the tenth, witnessed by Brother Oren. Coin in hand—"

He read the coin, the wages owed, the wages paid in scrip and the fourteen members who held it. Then he read the pledges one village at a time, all thirty-one, down to the hill village and its four gleams. The rider at the end of the row stood straighter when he heard his own.

"Received in pledges, one hundred and eighty gleams," Pell said. "Paid out for Lot 7, the Rain-Tithe of the Harrowdales, one hundred and sixty-four gleams. Grace remaining, twenty-five."

"Why tell us you're broke?" one of the factors called. His friends grinned.

"Because you already knew," Lys said. "Now you know by how much."

The dockers laughed, and it was not at him. He felt the forecourt tip a little, the way a scale tips when someone sets down the last small weight.

Oren unrolled the charter. The seal of Ilvenne sat at its foot, a wheat-sheaf pressed in something that was not wax and that shone faintly, of its own accord, in the flat noon light. He read the terms in his plain precise voice: the tithe of the Harrowdales held by the Cinderwick Company under the Harvest-Mother's witness; rain suspended one season pending tithe; rain to resume with the sowing in Mistmoon; for the villages who had pledged, a fixed rain-rent for ten years, as sworn.

Lys set two fingers on the parchment's edge.

The thread came at once — gold, clean and bright, running out of the scroll and up past the Temple's bronze scales into a sky he could not see the top of. It was the only thing in the forecourt that was entirely still. Behind his left eye a nail went in and turned. He held on for the count of three, long enough to be sure, then took his hand away and pressed the back of his wrist under his nose. It came away red. He folded his arms with the wrist hidden.

"Come and see," he told the riders. "Touch it if you like. It won't bite."

The stiff old elder came first, with help. He did not touch the scroll; he touched the Charter Stone, laying a cracked brown palm flat on the grey, and bent to the vein of light as Oren read it out to him — Standing, Tier, the lien, and beneath it the line that mattered: writs stayed until Emberwane 9 by right of divine charter held.

The old man straightened. "It says what the boy says," he said to the others.

It held. Mostly. Five of the seven riders went to find their mules for the long road home with the word. The sixth, a young man with a hard mouth, pulled the pledge-token off his neck, dropped it on the flagstones, and said they'd believe in the rain when it fell on them. The seventh stayed where he was, undecided, watching the factors leave.

Mira picked up the dropped token and put it in her pocket.

* * *

Mira found the barley on the seventeenth.

She had reasoned it the way she reasoned roads. Twelve hundred sacks was a barge-load, and a laden barge sits low and cannot hide long on a river with only so many berths. She had walked every wharf downstream of Mourngate for two days, asking, until a lock-keeper's boy pointed.

The barge lay at a downstream berth among the tanneries, broad-beamed and low in the water, with House Dain's lease-mark painted on the prow in blue and silver. Under a tarred sheet at the stern, the burlap showed a stencil: a cinder and a wick, the Company's mark, still sharp.

"We take it to the Guild Court," Mira said.

Oren, beside her on the wharf, nearly laughed. "The Guild Court is Concord men in borrowed robes. You would prove the barley was yours. They would find the thieves unknown, the lessee blameless, and award costs against you for troubling a noble house."

"Then we take it off the barge ourselves."

"And be the thieves in the telling," Lys said.

He looked at the stencil and felt the old appetite. In the other life he would have gone for the kill: evidence, filing, the counterparty's name in every paper by morning. But the other life had courts that did not dine with the defendant. And a man with a Seat and a face to keep, pinned in front of the whole river, could only do one thing afterwards: prove to everyone watching that he could not be pinned. Corner Aurel Dain and he would have to bite, because the whole Exchange would be waiting to see whether he did.

A win that bought you a war you couldn't afford was just a loss with better manners.

"Send for Crell," he said. "Tell him I'm on this wharf with a barge of his and Brother Oren's seal, and I'd like to discuss a neighbourly matter before the dockers' dinner hour."

"Before the dinner hour?" said Oren.

"When every docker on the river walks past this berth."

Crell came within the hour with two of his own men. By then forty dockers loitered on the wharf with their bread and cheese, in no hurry at all. Crell looked at the stencilled sacks, the crowd, Oren's seal. Lys watched him add it up.

"Master Vey."

"Master Crell. I'm afraid persons unknown robbed my warehouse on the tenth," Lys said, "and by a sad chance stowed what they took aboard a barge that House Dain happens to lease. I know Ser Aurel is as distressed as I am. You told me so on the thirteenth."

Crell said nothing. It was a very precise nothing.

"You also named a price for my barley that morning. Five marks the sack. I'd accept it for these twelve hundred — paid to Cinderwick as restitution, out of House Dain's neighbourly concern for a fellow guild in misfortune. Three hundred crowns. House Dain keeps the barley. Consider the sacks a gift." He paused. "The thieves, I expect, will stay unknown."

He watched Crell see it: the door left open. On this side, a noble house caught with stolen grain before forty dockers. On the other, a noble house paying generously to a struggling neighbour — at a price anyone on this wharf could hold against the Corn Hall board and understand perfectly.

"Payable by sundown," Crell said at last. "Under the Temple's witness, if Brother Oren will write it."

Oren wrote it on the barge's own capstan, in a hand like a row of nails. Crell signed. Lys signed. When Lys laid his thumb on the ink, the thread that rose from the paper was grey — a promise pending — and fine as a hair. The nail went back in behind his eye. He took his thumb away before his nose could betray him twice in one week.

At sundown a clerk in Dain's blue and silver brought a strongbox to Tallow Street and counted three hundred crowns onto the guildhall table. Lys touched the receipt. The grey thread had gone gold.

"You let him walk," Mira said, when the clerk had gone.

"I let him walk out through a door I chose. He paid three hundred crowns for barley worth a hundred and eighty and called it kindness, in front of half the river. Every docker on that wharf knows what it was. So does he."

"And next time?"

"Next time he has to come at me with something he doesn't mind being seen doing." He rubbed the ache behind his eye. "That's a shorter list."

The Stone, back on its footing and sullen about it, pulsed.

* * *

The courier came on the eighteenth, after dark, while Pell was entering the three hundred crowns in both columns and explaining to himself, under his breath, why they had to balance.

It was a child of seven or eight, barefoot, with a tin pin shaped like a lantern fixed to a shirt too big for her. She did not knock. She was simply inside the door, holding out a folded paper, and when Lys took it she was already gone into Tallow Street.

No seal. No signature. Good paper, a clerk's hand.

*The seven houses of the Gilded Concord have together sold sixty thousand sacks of grain for delivery on the first of Harvestmoon, at four marks the sack. House Dain alone owes twenty-five thousand.*

Lys read it twice.

Sixty thousand sacks at four marks. The Concord had taken the money for grain it did not own — sold the harvest before the harvest, at a glut price, to be delivered in a summer with no blessed rain on the valleys that grew four sacks in ten of everything Vessant ate. Every mark the Corn Hall price climbed before Harvestmoon would cost them sixty thousand marks. Three thousand crowns a mark. And the price had not climbed, because the Corn Hall had read Lot 7 as a dull line on a temple board and gone back to its slates.

So that was what silence was for.

Not his barley. Not even his charter, for itself. A drunk heir with a rain-tithe was a joke; a sober one telling the Harrowdales, with a god's seal, that no rain would fall before Mistmoon was a bell rung over the Corn Hall. The oil, the whispers, five marks for a charter — all of it to keep one fact dull until the first of Harvestmoon.

And four days ago he had stood in the Temple forecourt and had it read aloud to half the lower city.

He turned the paper over. On the back, in the same hand:

*The Lantern Market opens on the next moonless night. Bring your appetite. — V.*

Pell had stopped writing. Mira was watching him from the hearth.

"Well?" she said.

Lys folded the note along its creases and held it a moment longer than he needed to. Crell had told him the truth after all. Silence had a price. He had just found out what noise was worth — and exactly who would have to pay for it.
