# Chapter 6: Raising the Banner

Pell slapped the posting down on the counting table hard enough to make the candle jump.

"Forty words," he said. "I counted twice. *Lot twelve. The district charter of Low Wharrow, its warehouses, wharves, and rights of storage. Tier Two required. Bid bond twenty gleams.* That's the lot, Master Lys. That's all it says."

"That's what the temple posts," Lys said. "It isn't the charter."

Mira sat on the far end of the table with her boots on the bench, peeling a wrinkled apple with the knife she also used on rope. The scar through her eyebrow lifted. "It's a swamp with a roof on. Half a roof. The Concord takes it tomorrow for forty gleams, stacks their import grain in it, and nobody on that floor bids against them, because nobody on that floor wants a swamp."

"Sixty thousand sacks," Lys said.

"What?"

"That's what they have to put somewhere." He drew the Saltreach on the back of an old bill of lading, a wet line of ink curving past Mourngate. "They've sold sixty thousand sacks for delivery on Harvestmoon first, at four marks. They don't have them. So they buy outside the delta and float it down on the twenty-four barges we don't hold. Then it has to sit dry, near a wharf, for a month. Mourngate is ours. The upper granaries are full of their own old stock. That leaves one stretch of empty riverfront in Vessant." He tapped the line. "Low Wharrow."

The guildhall smelled the way it always smelled at night, of old tallow and older dust, with a thread of lamp oil from the lantern Pell had trimmed too high. The Charter Stone stood against the back wall, its vein of light pulsing slow and even. Below it sat the evening's Corn Hall figure, chalked by Pell on a board he had begged from a slate-cutter: barley, ten marks.

Lys caught himself rubbing his left wrist and made his hand stop.

Mira had seen. She put the apple down. "Every time that thing itches, you buy something."

"It isn't itching."

"Then why are you rubbing it?"

He did not answer that. At ten marks the barley at Mourngate was worth more than it had been since his father bought it, and each mark it climbed carried him closer to the number Korrath had written into his skin. Twelve. The brand did not care that the market had made him rich on paper. It cared about the poor-houses.

A knock, and a temple boy in a grey tabard held out a bundle tied with Sael's scale-knot. Lys paid him two bits and broke the knot. Three pages, closely written, in Oren's cramped hand, with a note pinned to the top:

*Copied from the archive. The posting is what they tell you. This is what you buy. — O.V.*

Lys read the first page standing. He read the second sitting down.

"What?" Pell said.

"Get your coat," Lys said. "And a better lantern. We're going to see a man about a wall."

* * *

Garrow Finch was waiting where the paving ended and the mud began, a shape so broad that Lys first took him for a mooring post. He carried no lantern. He did not seem to need one.

"Master Vey." The voice came from somewhere near the bottom of his chest. "You're late, and the tide isn't."

"I was reading."

"Aye. Well. The river's been reading too." Garrow turned and walked, and they followed.

Low Wharrow's warehouses still stood in their rows, long and blind, their loading doors nailed shut or hanging from one hinge. Grain dust and pigeon lime crusted the sills. Between them ran the levee, a bank of packed earth faced with dressed stone, higher than a man, keeping the river where the river was supposed to be.

It was not doing its job well.

Pell's lantern found the first seep fifty paces along. Water came through the face of the wall in a thin, patient sheet, black in the light, running down to pool in the lane. The mortar had gone to sand. A stone the size of a bread oven had slumped outward, and someone had shored it with a baulk of timber so rotten that Lys could push his thumb into it.

"That's Harlan's baulk," Garrow said. "Your father paid us to set it, eight years back, when he still thought he'd buy the district. He didn't buy it. Nobody bought it. The last holder let the charter lapse. Nobody's paid a docker to touch this wall since."

"How many breaches?"

"Like this?" Garrow considered. "Nine I'd call bad. Two I'd call a funeral if the autumn rains come heavy."

The autumn rains would come in early Mistmoon. Lys knew that better than anyone in the city. He had paid for some of them.

He unfolded Oren's copy against the dry side of a doorpost and held it to Pell's lantern. The charter was old; the language had the stiff, careful shape of something a god had once witnessed and a clerk had never since bothered to read.

*The holder shall keep the walls of Low Wharrow against the Saltreach, stone for stone, and shall answer before the Hammer for every sack, beam, and soul the river takes through neglect of them. Should the river enter by neglect, the charter falls forfeit, and the holder's Standing answers for the loss.*

He read it aloud. Pell's mouth came open.

"That's not storage," the boy said. "That's a debt."

"It's a warranty," Lys said, and then, because the word meant nothing here, "a promise to make good. Unlimited. Anyone who wins this lot and stacks sixty thousand sacks of someone else's grain behind a wall like that has sworn, before Sael, to pay for every one of them if the river comes in." He folded the pages. The headache that lived behind his left eye stirred without being asked; he had not touched a contract, but his body had begun to flinch at the language of them. "The Concord hasn't read it. They've read forty words."

"You're sure?" Garrow said.

"If they'd read it, they'd have been down here with masons last week. You'd have heard."

Garrow grunted.

"So you let them win it," Pell said. "Let them take the swamp and drown in it."

The thought had a clean shape, and a flaw underneath. "If Dain wins it, Dain doesn't mend the wall. He stacks the grain, delivers on Harvestmoon first, and lets the charter lapse before the rains. It's a month's storage to him. By the time the river comes in, the sacks are gone and nobody's left to answer." He looked at the black water sheeting down the stone, at the lane beyond, where a row of lean-tos housed the families of men who worked the wharves. A child's shoe sat in the mud by one of them. "Except the people who live here."

"And if you win it?" Garrow asked.

"Then I have to mend it. Which means I need the only men in Vessant who know this wall."

"Ah," said Garrow. "Now we come to it."

He sat down on a mooring bollard, which creaked, and waited. Lys had met him four times. The slow speech was not slowness. Garrow let silence do his bargaining for him, the way the Hammer did.

"The Brotherhood has grace," Lys said. "Oaths at the river shrine, fines kept, forty years of men swearing to haul true weights. Oren tells me it's near seventy gleams, pooled."

"Seventy-two."

"I want it pledged to Cinderwick for tomorrow. In return, every man of yours on the levee work and in the warehouses draws a mark a day, paid every Sael's Day, witnessed at the temple. And the Brotherhood holds one part in ten of Low Wharrow's storage rents for as long as we hold the charter."

Garrow looked at him for a long moment. Water dripped off the rotten baulk.

"Your father," he said, "owed my men for that baulk until the spring after he set it. Mira Thorne went four months unpaid. I hear things, Master Vey. I hear you settled your own teamsters in paper." He spread one enormous hand. "You're asking my brothers to give you the only thing they own that a god can see, and you're offering me a wage and a tenth. A wage is what you pay a mule."

"A wage paid on time is more than your men have had from anyone in this district."

"That's so. And it's a fine thing to say, standing on our wall." He did not raise his voice. "One part in four."

Lys felt the old reflex arrive, the one that came when he was frightened: reduce the man to a figure, find the figure's floor, press on it. Seventy-two gleams, a fixed labour cost, a slice of revenue. Garrow was a line on a sheet. He could see the sheet.

He could also see the child's shoe in the mud.

"One in five," he said. "Wages paid every Sael's Day, rain or none, even if the charter loses money. And one of your men sits in the counting room with Pell and reads the rent book whenever he likes."

Pell made a small noise of protest. Lys ignored it.

Garrow was quiet. Then he stood, and the bollard sighed with relief. "One of mine reads the book," he said. "I'd have asked for that last. You gave it first." He held out his hand. "One in five. We'll walk it to the temple before the Stair comes."

His palm was wet and hard as a hawser. No thread showed when they shook. It had not been witnessed yet. It would be.

* * *

The Temple of the Balance was full of lamps an hour before dawn, and Oren was full of ink. He had it to the wrist, and a smear across his forehead where he had pushed his hair back while converting the Brotherhood's pledge-tokens one by one at the altar. Garrow's men had come in their work clothes and knelt in their work clothes. The air smelled of wet rope and incense.

Veyra was waiting by the foot of the altar, where the Dawn Stair would begin. Her smoked-glass spectacles caught the lamplight and gave nothing back.

"You're going to have them read the small print," she said.

Lys kept his face still. "Who told you that?"

"Brother Oren went into the archive at supper. Archives have clerks. Clerks have mothers who like to eat." She held out a folded paper sealed with a lantern stamp. "Sixty gleams, Master Vey. Converted at the temple on the Syndicate's account at noon yesterday and waiting on your word. Against a claim on your first year of storage rents at Low Wharrow, at one part in six."

"One in six. The Brotherhood has one in five."

"Then the Company keeps a little over half." She smiled. "You'll manage. You're good with fractions."

He took the paper. Sixty gleams at a hundred and twenty crowns apiece was seven thousand two hundred crowns of coin that the Syndicate would never see again as coin. Veyra did not give things away.

"And if Dain wins," he said, "you'll offer him the same sixty to cover his storage losses when the river comes in."

"I would never lie to you," Veyra said, which was not a denial. "Go on. The sky's getting light."

Back at Tallow Street, while the Stair was still gathering itself, he laid his palm on the Charter Stone and felt the pledges settle in. Fifty-two gleams of the Company's own, scraped from four months of kept rain-shares and tithes. Seventy-two from the Brotherhood. Sixty from the lanterns. The vein brightened under his hand.

One hundred and eighty-four. He decided, there, with his hand on the stone, what the district was worth to him, and that he would not pay a gleam more. A hundred and sixty. Past that, Dain could have it and its wall.

* * *

The Exchange was cold at dawn, the marble floor white as salt, the clouds lying flat below its edge like a second, softer floor. There were no walls. There was the gallery of forty carved Seats, and above them, the gods.

They stood tall and masked and silent. Sael's mask was a plain disc of silver, the Hammer resting across its forearms.

Cinderwick's Seat, the thirty-ninth, had been freshly scrubbed. Across the curve of the gallery sat the Concord in silk: Oskel in dark red, Merrow and Hale side by side, Castellane, Ruske, Vantheil in dark green. Ser Aurel Dain sat in the Speaker's Seat with his silver hair combed back and his hands folded, and inclined his head to Lys as courteously as if they had never exchanged a note of forty-one thousand crowns across a counting table.

The lots before twelve went quickly: a tithe on a fishing weir, a blessing of sound timber for a shipwright's yard. The Hammer rang each time with that sound like a bell heard from under water.

"Lot twelve," called the temple crier. "The district charter of Low Wharrow."

Lys stood. "Seat thirty-nine requires the lot read in its full terms before the first bid."

The floor went quiet in a way it had not been quiet all morning. Somewhere in the Concord's rows a man laughed once, stopped. Oren, standing at the lectern below the gods in his faded steward's robe, did not look up. He had been waiting.

"The rule is old," Oren said, to the whole floor, "but it has never been struck. Any Seat may require it."

Sael's mask turned, a fraction. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, and was very short.

"Read."

Oren read. He read all three pages without hurrying, in the flat, careful tone he used for weights and measures, and when he came to *every sack, beam, and soul the river takes*, he did not change his tone at all. That was worse. Lys watched the words land. The Castellane factor put his pen down. The Ruske delegate's clerk began to count on his fingers.

When Oren finished, nobody spoke for three heartbeats. Nobody had bid, so the Hammer did not fall; it only waited.

"Forty gleams," Lys said.

He let the headache in.

It came like a nail tapped once behind the eye, and the room changed. At Tier Two the Bind showed him every witnessed contract on the floor, and the Exchange was nothing but witnessed contracts. Grey threads ran from every Seat to the Hammer: the bid bonds, twenty gleams each, pending. Gold threads ran up into the masks, charters long held and long kept. It was a loom. He made himself look for the pattern and nothing else.

Dain's bond did not run alone. Two grey threads came into it from the side, braided in: one from Merrow's Seat, one from Vantheil's. Pledges. Dain was not bidding his own grace. He was bidding a committee's.

Committees, in Lys's old life, folded at round numbers. Nobody wanted to be the one who broke the ceiling set in the back room.

"Fifty," said Hale.

"Sixty," said Vantheil.

"Seventy," Dain said pleasantly.

Castellane stayed silent. Ruske's clerk stopped counting and shook his head. Oskel had never bid at all; Lady Benedetta sat with her chin on her hand and watched Lys with open interest.

"Seventy-five," Lys said.

"Ninety," said Vantheil.

Hale dropped. Lys saw the Hale delegate sit back and fold his arms, and he saw Merrow's man glance at Dain, and Dain give him nothing.

"Ninety-five."

"A hundred," Dain said.

The warm wet of the nosebleed began. He pressed a knuckle under his nostril and kept his voice even. "A hundred and five."

He had learned on this floor, on the day of the Rain-Tithe, what a jump bid said. Today he wanted to say the opposite. Small steps, quick, bored. *I will be here all morning. I have nowhere else to be.* Every five gleams he added cost the Concord five gleams of grace they had pledged to a lot they had meant to steal for forty. Every five cost him five as well, but his five came with a wall he already had men to mend.

"A hundred and twenty," Vantheil said, and there was an edge in it now.

"A hundred and twenty-five."

The Vantheil delegate looked at Dain. It was brief. Lys saw it, and saw the grey thread from Vantheil's Seat into Dain's bond, pulled taut across the floor like a line with a fish on it. Vantheil's pledge ran to Dain. Vantheil was bidding against his own money.

The delegate sat back. Vantheil was out.

"A hundred and thirty," Dain said.

"A hundred and thirty-five."

"A hundred and forty."

"A hundred and forty-five."

Blood was on his lip now. Pell, in the clerks' rank below, passed up a clean rag without looking at him.

"A hundred and fifty," said Ser Aurel Dain, and the number sat on the marble, round and handsome, and Lys watched the Merrow delegate stop breathing.

A hundred and fifty was the ceiling. He would have bet the Company on it. He did not need to; he had already set his own ceiling, ten gleams above, and this was the part where he found out whether he had meant it.

He had. Past a hundred and sixty he would sit down, and Dain could have his district and his wall and his neighbourly concern, and in Mistmoon, when the river came in, Dain would learn what *every soul* cost.

"A hundred and fifty-five," Lys said.

One heartbeat. Dain's hands stayed folded.

Two. The Merrow delegate looked down at his own knees.

Three.

The Hammer fell, and the sound went through the marble and up through Lys's boots and into the cracked place behind his eye, and for a breath the loom of threads blazed and he could not see anything at all.

"Seat thirty-nine," the crier called.

When his eyes cleared, Aurel Dain was looking at him across the curve of the gallery. The Speaker of the Concord did not frown. He lifted two fingers from his folded hands, a small salute between men who kept accounts, and turned to speak to his factor.

That was the thing Lys would remember, climbing down. Not the Hammer. The two fingers.

"Four thousand one hundred," Pell said behind him, reverent.

"It's a wall," Lys said. "Tell the masons."

* * *

They raised the banner at dusk.

Garrow's men had cut the pole from the mast of a barge that would never float again, and they set it on the roof of the tallest warehouse at the end of the levee, where the Saltreach bent and anyone coming down the river would see it first. The cloth had been in a chest in the guildhall since Harlan's time: a black field, a cinder in red thread, a wick in gold rising from it. Moths had been at one corner. Nobody had mended it. Lys had decided nobody would.

Hobb hauled the line himself, bad knee and all. When the banner caught the wind and opened, the dockers along the lane below roared, a big, rough, unmusical sound that bounced off the blind warehouse walls and came back doubled. Someone started banging a pot.

Mira stood beside Lys at the roof's edge and did not cheer. She watched the banner, and then she watched the dockers, and then the families in the lean-tos who had come out to see what the shouting was about. Her mouth did something complicated.

"My mother lived three streets from here," she said. "Before the wall went the first time."

He had not known that. The body's smudged memory offered nothing.

"We'll mend it before Mistmoon," he said.

"I know you will." She looked at him sidelong, the scarred eyebrow crooked. "That's what's strange."

Below, at the edge of the crowd, a neat figure in a dark coat stood apart from the pot-banging, a ledger under his arm. Crell. He did not climb. He sent a boy up the ladder with a letter sealed in House Dain's wax, and the boy handed it to Mira, who broke the seal and read it by the last of the light.

"House Dain requests terms," she said, dry as chalk. "Storage for twenty-five thousand sacks at Low Wharrow, Harvestmoon first. And barge hire. Forty sailings."

Lys took it. It was courteous. Everything from that house was courteous. A house that had meant to own this roof was asking to rent a corner of it.

The rest of the Concord would follow. Seven houses, sixty thousand sacks promised, and every sack of it would now have to come down Cinderwick's river on Cinderwick's barges and rest behind Cinderwick's wall before it could keep their word for them.

He should have felt it as a win. He did, for a moment. He let himself.

Then his left wrist caught fire.

Not an itch. Not heat. The Oathbrand flared as if someone had pressed a coal into the skin and held it there, and the breath went out of him, and he gripped his own forearm hard enough to bruise. Mira's hand closed on his shoulder.

"Lys—"

Feet on the ladder. Pell had run for the Corn Hall's evening chalk the moment the banner was up, and was coming back faster than the ladder deserved, the little slate under his arm and grain dust grey in his hair.

"Evening chalk," he said, gasping. He turned the slate so they could read it in the dusk. "Barley eleven. Bread-grain up a mark and a half since noon."

Eleven.

Below them the dockers were singing, badly, about a river and a girl. Somewhere in the poor-houses of the lower town, tomorrow's mark would buy less bread than today's.

Lys did the sum without wanting to. At twelve marks the covenant would call in twenty thousand sacks at four. Mourngate held sixteen thousand eight hundred, after the theft. The rest he would have to buy on the open floor at twelve, from houses that had just watched him take their district, and sell at four to people who could not afford one.

One mark from the trigger. A day, perhaps two.

He looked at the banner, black and red and gold against the last of the light, and at the brand on his wrist, a red welt that pulsed in time with his heart, and understood that he had spent the morning buying the only warehouse in Vessant big enough to hold the grain he was about to be forced to give away.

"Pell," he said. "Tomorrow, before the chalk goes up, I want every sack in Mourngate counted by hand. Every one."

"We counted in Highsun."

"Count again." He watched the dockers below, the families in the doorways, the child whose shoe he had seen in the mud last night now wearing it, running. "And find out what bread costs in the poor-houses. Not the Corn Hall price. What they're actually paying."

"Why?"

Because within days a god was going to ask him whether a promise was a liability to be managed or a debt to be paid, and he did not yet know which answer he was going to give.

"Because I want to know," he said, "what I've sworn to."
