# Chapter 2: The First Charter

The horse came down Tallow Street at a walk that had stopped being a walk somewhere south of the city and become a slow, controlled fall. Lys heard it before he saw it: shoes dragging on wet cobbles, a loose stirrup knocking, and a woman cursing in the flat, economical way of someone who had run out of better words two days ago.

He was on the guildhall step with the lamp before Pell had finished saying her name.

Mira Thorne did not dismount so much as allow the ground to have her. She held the saddle for a count of three, then let go of it as though it had insulted her. Road dust had turned her hair the colour of old rope; the scar through her eyebrow stood out white against a face scoured raw by five days of wind.

"Water," she said. "Then questions. Then I'm sleeping on your floor, Vey, and if anyone wakes me before noon I'll kill them."

"The auction's at dawn."

"Then I'll kill them after."

Pell ran for the water. Lys took the bridle and watched her unbuckle a satchel from the saddle-bow as a porter handles a cask of lamp oil near an open flame. When she swung it onto the step it clattered like kindling.

Inside, the hall smelled of tallow gone stale in the beams, and of what Lys had brought into it over five days: ink, a great deal of it. The long table was buried under Pell's scrip-rolls, one for each of the fourteen members who had taken a witnessed stake in place of a wage. Two of the signatures were thumbprints. One was a careful drawing of a cart.

Mira drank three cups of water, then upended the satchel over the scrip.

Tally sticks. Dozens of them, hazel and ash, each split lengthwise so its notches would match the half left behind in some village shrine. Wax sealed each end with the wheat-sheaf of Ilvenne. Some were bound with red thread. One was wrapped in a child's hair ribbon.

His hands were already sorting, by seal, by notch-count. The part of him that had spent sixteen years in London reading other people's disasters off spreadsheets woke and reached for a pen.

Lys read the notches aloud. Pell chalked them on a board across his knees. When the last stick went onto the last pile, the boy's lips moved over the column twice, and he looked up with the gap in his teeth showing.

"A hundred and eighty gleams. Thirty-one villages."

"With our nine," Lys said, "a hundred and eighty-nine." Twenty for the bid bond. The rest a ceiling, and a ceiling was only useful if you knew how far below it to stand. "Oren has to walk these through the temple tonight or they're sticks. Pell, your coat. Mira—"

"You haven't asked."

He looked up. She had not sat down. She stood at the end of the table with her arms folded and the lamplight making her older.

"How it went," she said. "What they said. Whether anyone set dogs on me. You've counted those sticks twice and you haven't once asked."

"I assumed if it had gone badly there'd be fewer sticks."

It was the wrong answer. He knew it the moment it was out, watched it land in the set of her jaw.

"Fewer sticks." She picked out the one with the ribbon. "Old woman at the head of the last valley. Her son farms the high terraces, where the dry bites first. She gave me three gleams. You know what three gleams is to a hill village? That's their shrine. Forty years of weddings and every handshake that shrine ever witnessed, scraped into a stick, because I told her the rain would come back."

"Because we told her."

"Because *I* told her." Mira set the stick down on its own, apart from the pile. "They don't know you. Down there, your name's the punchline to a story about your father's wake. They know me. Eleven years I've hauled their grain up that road and never shorted a sack. So when they asked who stood behind the promise, I didn't say Cinderwick." She tapped her own breastbone, once. "I swore on my name. At every shrine. Thirty-one times."

The terms were his, drafted with Oren on the back of a bill of lading: the villages pledge grace now; if Cinderwick wins, they pay a fixed rain-rent, lower than anything Oskel ever charged, for ten years. If Cinderwick loses, the grace goes back to the shrines. A pre-sale. Customers funding the mill before it was built, for a price they could hold you to.

He had not drafted a clause for Mira's name. In his old life that had a label too: personal guarantee. He had read hundreds of them and thought of them, when he thought of them at all, as a line item.

The line item was standing across the table with road grit in the creases of her eyes.

"If there's no rain," she said, "I don't lose four months' wages. I lose the road. I can't ride south of the Saltreach again without someone spitting in my horse's water."

"Then we'd better win."

"That's it?"

Thank you would have been true, and would have sounded like the thing people said to close a meeting.

"I'm sorry I counted first."

"No, you're not." There was no heat left in it, only tiredness. "You'd count first again tomorrow. Just know what it is you're counting." She took the ribbon stick back off its own pile and put it with the others, notches up. "Go on. Wake your priest. I'll be on the floor."

* * *

Brother Oren Vale was not asleep. Lys had begun to suspect he never was. They found him in the nave of the Temple of the Balance trimming wicks beneath the great brass scales, and he looked at the satchel in Pell's arms the way a physician looks at a wound that has turned out exactly as bad as predicted.

"All of them?"

"Thirty-one villages. A hundred and eighty gleams."

Oren set down the snuffer and held out his hands.

The rite was slower than Lys had imagined, and less mystical. Oren read each seal, spoke the village's name and the notch-count into the quiet, and laid the stick in the right-hand pan. Nothing went into the left. The beam dipped all the same, as though something on the far side had leaned down to check the figure, and when Oren lifted the stick again the wax had gone from red to the grey of old candle-ends.

Lys picked up the next one before Oren could reach it.

Pain arrived first, as it always did: a nail driven in behind his left eye. Then the thread. It came out of the stick in his palm, fine and grey, a pending thing, and ran away through the temple wall, south, down the mountain's flank and out over the delta toward valleys he had never seen. At its far end a shrine held the other half. A woman who did not know his face was trusting it anyway.

He let go. Something warm ran over his lip, and he wiped it with his wrist before Oren could remark on it. Oren remarked on it anyway, with one eyebrow.

It took most of an hour. When the last stick had greyed, Oren led them into the vestry, where forty discs of smoked glass hung in brass frames along the wall, one for every Seat, each holding a faint vein of light like a coal seen through fog. The temple's tally-glasses. Oren unhooked the one marked with a cinder and a wick.

Pell whooped under his breath. Lys read it twice.

"Standing didn't move."

"Why would it?" Oren slid the glass into his sleeve. "Sael weighs what is yours. That isn't. It's theirs, lent to you for one morning on your promise and hers."

An asset with a matching liability. The Stone kept honest books. Lys found, absurdly, that this comforted him more than the number did.

"Understand me, Master Vey," Oren said. "I think your reasoning about the rain is sound. But you are about to carry thirty-one shrines up the Stair and spend them on a lot every house on that floor thinks is a dead field. If you're wrong, I explain to thirty-one villages why their grace bought them nothing."

"It goes back to them if we lose."

"The grace does. Faith is not refunded. Do you know what you'll pay?"

"I know what it's worth to everyone else."

He laid it out on the walk to the doors, because saying it aloud was the only way to find the holes. To the great houses, Lot 7 was a field with no rain this season, so no rain-rent, so a year of upkeep before a copper came back. They would price it like a lame horse.

"And Lady Oskel?" Oren said. "It was hers."

"She'll want it back. But she lost it for failing to pay the tithe, and a house that can't pay a tithe has no grace to spare. Nor can she be seen chasing it with her own hand. The Concord would smell it." Lys stopped on the top step, where the river smell came up cold off Scale Street. "So she'll bid through someone else, with someone else's grace. Someone always lends to a proud house. The question is who, and at what price."

* * *

The Dawn Stair did not rise. It was simply there, between one breath and the next, as the first grey light touched the temple forecourt: white stone steps beginning at the altar-door and climbing into cloud.

A Sael's Day crowd had gathered to watch: carters bound for the Corn Hall, a baker's boy with a tray of rolls. Pell stayed with them, clutching the satchel of empty tally-halves like a relic. Lys set his foot on the first step and felt the stone hum through his boot-sole.

Oren went first, because he knew the pace. Mira came last, jaw set against sleep; nobody had managed to keep her on the floor. The city dropped away. Tallow Street became a crease, the Saltreach a brown ribbon laid through mudflats and warehouses. Mourngate squatted on the bank like a toad over eighteen thousand sacks no one wanted. Then the cloud closed, cold and tasting of iron, and for a long time there was nothing but white and the next step.

They came out of it into sunlight so clean it hurt.

The Exchange was a floor of veined marble laid on top of the clouds, two hundred paces across, with no walls and nothing at its edges but a drop into brilliance. Around the far half ran a crescent gallery of forty carved Seats, each a high-backed stone chair on its own dais. Silks moved on the near Seats, blue and bronze and oxblood: the Concord houses settling in with the ease of people who had never climbed that Stair afraid.

At the centre of the floor stood the gods.

Three of them this morning, taller than anything with a body ought to be. Each wore a smooth, faintly luminous mask, and robes that did not stir in the high wind. The one in the centre held a hammer of dark bronze no larger than a fist. The one at its left wore a mask the pale gold of new straw, and the air near her smelled, impossibly, of wet earth. The third stood apart, hands folded, a ring on every finger.

Sael the Weigher. Ilvenne the Harvest-Mother. Korrath, come to witness.

Lys's borrowed memories told him not to stare. He stared until Oren steered him toward the far end of the crescent.

Seat 39 was the second-last chair. Its arms were carved with a cinder and a wick, the right one worn smooth by twenty years of his father's hand. Lys did not sit.

"Master Vey."

Ser Aurel Dain had come along the gallery alone, in grey so fine it looked like smoke. His hair was the same colour. He was smiling, and the smile reached his eyes; Lys had watched enough men fake that across boardroom tables to know this one wasn't faked.

"Ser Aurel."

"Crell tells me you've given up wine."

"It gave me up."

"Mm." Dain considered him as one considers a horse reported lame that now appears to be standing. "He tells me you read the writ properly. Every line. Most men only read the sum." He glanced at the carved cinder. "Your father sat there twenty years. I was fond of him. He bid like a man who believed the sky would always be generous."

"It usually is, for the people sitting at your end."

Mira, behind him, made a very small sound.

Dain only nodded, as though Lys had made a fair point about the weather. "The Guild Court sits on the thirteenth. I'll be there, of course. I would so much rather be there as a friend of the family." He inclined his head to Oren, then to Mira, whose name he did not use and plainly knew. "Whatever you bid for this morning, Master Vey, be careful what you win."

He walked back up the gallery to Seat 2 and sat down in it without looking round.

"Friend of the family," Mira said.

"He means it," Lys said. That was the unpleasant part.

* * *

Oren took him to the bond-rail, a waist-high bar of bronze at the floor's edge. Lys laid his palm on it, and twenty gleams left him like warmth leaving a bath, a slow draining under the breastbone. When he stepped back his hands were cold.

The first six lots went in the time it took the sun to clear the cloud-line. A blessing on a bakers' guild's ovens, eleven gleams. The fishing tithe of a creek no one had heard of, to House Ruske for the bond and one gleam more. An acolyte in white read each lot at Sael's feet; the Seats answered in plain numbers; and each lot ended the same way, with a silence three heartbeats long, and then the Hammer came down on nothing and the sound was a bell rung under deep water.

"Sold," said Sael, and that was all the god ever said.

"Lot seven," the acolyte read. "The Rain-Tithe of the Harrowdales. Forfeit by House Oskel. Rebid. Rain suspended one season pending tithe."

A murmur went round the crescent, bored, faintly amused. Somebody near the middle laughed.

"Ten," said a clerk from one of the lower Seats.

"Fifteen."

"Twenty," from House Merrow's factor, with the air of a man bidding to keep awake.

It crept to thirty. Lys stood with his hands behind his back and watched faces instead of the lot: who was bidding for sport, who for a ledger, who for someone else.

Then a woman spoke from Seat 22.

"Forty."

She wore grey wool, plain as a clerk's. Seat 22 belonged to a sailmakers' guild so small Lys's borrowed memory could not supply its name. The woman was no sailmaker. She held her bidding hand at her collar, and under her fingers, catching the light, was a small pin shaped like a lantern.

"There," Oren breathed.

Lys was looking past her, up the crescent, to Seat 6. Lady Benedetta Oskel sat very straight in dark red, hands folded in her lap, not one finger moving. She was not watching the woman in grey. She was carefully, expensively not watching her.

"Forty-eight," said House Vantheil.

"Fifty-five," said the woman.

"Sixty."

"Sixty-four."

Vantheil went to seventy-two and sat back, a man who had bought a cheap chance and would be pleased either way. Merrow had dropped. Two bidders left, and one of them was a stranger bidding somebody else's grace for somebody else's pride, with a ceiling Lys could not see.

He could guess at it. Whoever had lent to Oskel had lent against a figure, and the woman in grey would not be allowed past it. If he climbed toward it one gleam at a time, she would climb beside him, each small step telling her he was as tired as she was.

So he would not climb.

"One hundred and twenty," Lys said.

The number fell into the thin air like a dropped plate. At Seat 2, Ser Aurel Dain lifted his gaze from his folded hands for the first time.

A jump bid. In his old life it had a name and a literature. Here it was simpler: a man who leaps forty-eight gleams at once does not care about forty-eight gleams, and a man who does not care about forty-eight gleams might have three hundred behind him. A lie told entirely with the truth. It had cost him forty-odd gleams he might never have needed to spend. He had bought the room's belief with them.

Vantheil's factor shook his head and looked at the sky.

The woman in grey let two heartbeats pass. On the second, her eyes went — not to Lys, but a finger's width up the crescent, toward Seat 6. Lady Oskel was studying her folded hands.

"One hundred and twenty-five."

Five. Not ten, not twenty. A step that small was a step taken by someone counting.

"One hundred and thirty," said Lys.

"One hundred and thirty-eight."

"One hundred and forty-five."

"One hundred and fifty." A beat. "Two."

Her voice had not changed. Her hand at her collar had. The fingers had closed around the lantern pin.

He had a hundred and eighty-nine, the bond counted toward whatever he won. He could say it. Either she would top it and he would go home with returned grace and Mira's burned road, or she would fold and he would hold the Rain-Tithe on an empty Stone, and every house on the floor would know how deep his pockets went, because he had emptied them in public.

And in Mistmoon, when the rain came back, the upkeep would start at twenty gleams a month.

A hundred and eighty-nine meant *all I have*. He did not want to say that number on this floor, to these people, ever.

"One hundred and sixty-four," said Lys.

Twelve. A bigger step than hers, from a man who, by the look of it, still wasn't tired. Twenty-five gleams under the ceiling. Twenty for the first month the rain was owed, five for everything he hadn't thought of yet.

The silence began.

One. The woman in grey drew breath. Her lips parted on a number.

Two. Her eyes went to Seat 6. Lady Oskel had turned her head away entirely, toward the brilliant nothing at the floor's edge, still as her carved chair.

Three.

The Hammer came down, and the bell rang under the water and through Lys's ribs.

"Sold," said Sael.

* * *

Mira's hand closed on his shoulder hard enough to bruise. Oren bowed his head toward the gods, lips moving, and Lys did not ask what he was saying.

The acolyte brought the charter across the marble on a tray of plain wood: a scroll thicker than vellum, bound with a plait of green-gold straw that was still, faintly, alive. Lys knew what would happen when he touched it. He touched it anyway.

The nail went in behind his eye, deeper than at the temple.

Gold.

A single thread as thick as a finger and bright as coin in sunlight, and it did not run south to the valleys. It ran up. Straight from his hand to the straw-pale mask of the Harvest-Mother, and it was warm, and it was *kept*. A promise made, sealed and witnessed, and the other party to it was a god.

From the same knot, fainter, thirty-one grey threads fanned away over the floor's edge and down through the cloud. Pending. They would stay grey until Mistmoon, until the first rain fell on the first dry field. He wanted, with an intensity that surprised him, to be there when they turned.

A cloth appeared under his nose. Mira's.

"You're bleeding on the god's paper," she said.

He let go. The gold vanished; the ache stayed. He pressed the cloth to his face and felt the rest of the grace go, not the bond's slow draining this time but one decisive pull, a hundred and forty-four gleams on top of the twenty at the rail. His knees wanted to sit down. He did not let them.

Oren had the tally-glass out of his sleeve.

"Ninety days," Oren said quietly. "Under Ilvenne's hand. No writ of the Guild Court can touch your Seat until the ninth of Emberwane."

Five hundred and eighty-eight. The Stone had counted what he now held, and a kept contract with a god, and nearly trebled him. Still Tin. Still the second-last chair. But the thirteenth of Seedmoon had just stopped being the day he died.

* * *

They went down with the lesser guilds while the Concord lingered on the floor. The cloud was thinner now, shot through with sun. Halfway into it, a voice at Lys's shoulder said, "May I?"

Dain fell into step without waiting for an answer. Mira dropped back two paces, close enough to hear. Oren went on ahead, not quite fast enough to be out of earshot.

"Congratulations. Sincerely," Dain said. "That leap at seventy-two especially. Vantheil will be describing it to his wife tonight." Mist beaded on his grey sleeves and he did not brush it off. "May I offer an observation, as a friend of your father's?"

"I doubt I could stop you."

"You've bought rain for a desert, Master Vey." He said it gently, as a physician might mention a shadow on the lung. "And you've told every house on that floor that your guild has a plan. Plans can be read."

He nodded, pleasant as a neighbour, and lengthened his stride, and the cloud took him.

Lys stood on the Stair with the bloodied cloth in his fist and the charter under his arm, and let the cold get into him.

Every house on that floor had watched a bankrupt heir with nine gleams to his name spend a hundred and sixty-four. They had not seen thirty-one villages. They had seen grace appear from nowhere, and a drunk who bid like a counting-house. By noon every one of them would be asking where the Vey boy found a hundred and eighty gleams, and what he knew about the Harrowdales that they didn't.

Dain, he thought, had already started answering.

And the note had not moved. The stay stopped the writ; it did not touch the debt. Dain's paper for forty-one thousand crowns sat in his strongbox exactly where it had sat at dawn, and when the stay lifted on the ninth of Emberwane it would still be there, with his hand resting on it. The clock had not stopped. It had been wound, and now ran ninety days instead of four.

Twenty-five gleams. Two hundred and twelve crowns, less five days of lamp oil. A granary of barley at three marks a sack, and a charter for rain that would not fall until Mistmoon. Behind him on the Stair, a woman whose name was now tied to his like a hawser to a bollard.

Plans can be read. Fine. The question was not whether Dain would read this one.

It was what Dain would do with it before Lys had written the next page.
