# Chapter 10: A Realm in Brass

The headwoman pushed the clay cup into his hands before he could refuse it, and Lys drank, because refusing water in a dry valley was a debt no ledger could settle.

It tasted of iron and the bottom of the well.

The field ran out to the hedgerows in pale stubble and cracked furrows, the soil broken into plates the size of his palm. Thirty-odd people stood along its edge: elders from the nearer villages, their daughters and sons, a boy holding a goat on a rope. None of them were looking at him. They were looking south-west, over the ridge, where the sky was the colour of old linen and had been for four months.

Mira stood at his shoulder with her reins looped over one forearm. Road dust had settled in the scar through her eyebrow. "You keep checking the sun."

"Ilvenne doesn't keep hours."

"Neither do you, apparently. That's nine times."

"Eight."

It was Mistmoon 2. The charter said the rain would resume in Mistmoon. It did not say which day, and Lys had spent the two-day ride south reading Oren's gloss in the margins until the words went to felt. *Suspended one season pending tithe.* The season was over. The first month's upkeep, twenty gleams to the Harvest-Mother, had been posted on Scale Street before they left, and Pell had filed the receipt with the corner folded so it could be found in the dark.

Everything was paid. That was the difficulty with paying everything. Afterwards there was nothing to do but stand in a field and find out whether the other party was honest.

The headwoman took back her cup. She was small and brown as a walnut, and she had been the first elder to put a pledge-token into Mira's hand in Seedmoon, on nothing but Mira's name. The token hung at her throat now on a greasy cord, a disc of fired clay stamped with the cinder-and-wick.

"My grandson says the Company will raise the rain-rent once the rain comes," she said. "He says that's how it goes with city men. Well's dry, you promise cheap. Well's full, you remember what water is worth."

The boy with the goat went red to the ears.

"Your grandson has been reading the Corn Hall slates," Lys said. "He's right about city men."

"And you?"

"Ten years at the rent stamped on your token. It's witnessed. If I change it, the Oathkeeper takes the difference out of me." He turned his left wrist so she could see the brand, pink and ridged under the cuff. "He's already taken a deposit."

She studied it longer than was polite. Then her eyes went past him to the ridge, and Lys felt it before he saw it: a coolness on the back of his neck, like a cellar door opening in a hot room.

The cloud came over the ridge low and fast and grey-green at the belly. The smell reached them before the water did. Wet dust, stone, crushed grass. Theo had a word for that smell, a clever word from another life, and it would not come; Lys found he did not need it.

The first drop struck the back of his hand. Then the soil, dark spots the size of bits, then too many to count.

Nobody cheered. A woman knelt and set both palms flat on the ground as if taking its pulse. The goat bawled. The headwoman began to pray, not loudly, to Ilvenne first, and then, which he had not expected, to Korrath and to Sael: the god of oaths and the god of measure. The gods of a bargain kept.

Behind his breastbone, the Bind pulled taut.

He knew the feeling now. It was the thread that ran from him to the Charter Stone on Tallow Street, two days north, and it was suddenly carrying traffic. He let the Ledger-sight open a crack. Every pledge-token in the field had worn a grey thread since Seedmoon, dull as pewter, trailing away north. As the rain soaked the cords they went gold, one after another, like lamps being lit down a street.

The nail went in behind his left eye. He shut the sight before it could go deeper.

An old man with a split thumbnail pushed through to him, holding out a purse. It clinked with real weight. "For the Company," he said. "Over the rent. For keeping faith."

Lys looked at the purse. Low Wharrow's levees ate coin by the cartload, and the Vault's sixty-six thousand crowns would come due at the end of next year whether it rained or not. The number on the purse would not be large. It would not be nothing.

"The rent's the rent," he said.

"It's a gift."

"Then give it to her." He nodded at the woman with her hands in the mud. "Or to Ilvenne. Not to me. If I take more than we swore, your grandson was right, and next year somebody's grandson believes him."

The old man stood with the purse held out until his arm tired. Then he laughed, short and surprised, and closed his fist on it, and turned to the woman in the mud.

The Bind pulled again. Harder.

Mira did not speak until they were mounted and the road had turned to slurry under the hooves. "Three months ago you'd have taken that and written him a receipt."

"Three months ago I needed it."

"You need it now."

"Not that coin." He wiped rain out of his eyes. His hand came away pink; the nosebleed had started quietly while he wasn't watching. "That coin costs more than it pays."

She looked at him the way she looked at a bridge she was not sure of, and rode on.

* * *

They came up Tallow Street before dawn on Mistmoon 4, soaked to the thigh, and found the guildhall lit.

The hall smelled as it always had, old tallow and ink and wet wool, but there was a new note under it, a hum Lys felt in his back teeth before he reached the door. Pell was sitting on the ledger bench in his shirt, knees drawn up, staring at the Charter Stone.

"It woke me," Pell said. "Day before yesterday, about noon. It's been going ever since. I didn't touch it."

The vein of light in the grey stone was not a vein any longer. It ran the whole height of the block, white-gold, and the letters stood out of it as though cut fresh.

```text
⟦ CINDERWICK COMPANY — CHARTER STONE ⟧
STANDING: 20,640 (Tier IV — Sovereign House)
SEAT 39 — HELD
GRACE: 1,212 gleams
SOVEREIGN CHARTERS — OPEN TO BID
```

Mira read it over his shoulder, lips moving. Hobb had come in behind them, dragging his bad knee, and said nothing at all, which from Hobb was a speech.

Lys did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was what he had instead of prayer. Thirty-one villages on their knees in the mud, thanking three gods for a promise kept. The Stone had crossed twenty thousand somewhere on the road north while he was keeping a horse out of a ditch.

At Tier III he could read strain: which contracts in a room were fraying. What he wanted was simpler. He wanted to know whether any of the thirty-one had failed to turn: a village where the rain had missed, a token still grey. If one had, somebody would say by week's end that Cinderwick had sold them sky and delivered dust, and he had seen what a rumour could do in Seedmoon.

He could not see the Harrowdales from Tallow Street. He knew that. He opened the sight anyway, carefully, the way one tests ice.

The room was not there.

The city was.

Every witnessed contract in Vessant stood out of the dark at once, as if someone had lifted the roof off the world and the streets were drawn in wire. The sixty barge leases on the Saltreach, a gold ladder down the river. The poor-house covenant, gold, thick as a hawser, knotted into the lower city. Low Wharrow's levee oaths, grey and gold and a few, near the old sluice, flushed dull red. Ten thousand grey threads in the Corn Hall, the day's small bargains, pending. Up in the high city, where House Dain stood, a snarl of red-black like a burnt hand. And past all of it, threads running up the mountain to the Exchange, and a few, very few, running past the Exchange into a height he had no name for.

It came in all at once, and it did not stop coming.

He did not remember going down. He remembered the flagstones, cold against his cheek, and Mira's fist in his collar, and Pell's voice counting slowly to ten and starting again. There was blood in his mouth. Something in the back of his skull had been pulled and not yet let go.

"Lys." Mira's voice. "How many fingers?"

"You're not holding any up."

"Good. He's back." Her grip did not ease. "What did you do?"

He considered lying and found he had no appetite for it. "I looked at the Harrowdales. The Stone showed me Vessant instead. All of it."

Pell had gone to the Stone and was reading the last line again, under his breath. "Sovereign charters. That's land, isn't it? Land with a lord on it."

"Land with a lord on it," Lys said to the ceiling beams. "We can bid for it now."

"And every house left on that floor knows you can," said Mira.

Lys lay still and let the nosebleed run into his ear. That, too, was a line on the Stone, if you knew how to read it.

* * *

Ser Aurel Dain arrived at the third bell of the afternoon, on foot, and waited on the step.

He did not knock. Hobb found him there, standing under the drip from the lintel where Crell had nailed the Tenth-Day Writ in Seedmoon, with rain darkening the shoulders of a plain grey coat. Lys went out to him. He had meant to make Dain come in and sit in the chair across the counting table, and when he saw the coat he changed his mind and did not know why.

"Master Vey. You're pale."

"I've been in the Harrowdales. It rained."

"So I'm told." Dain's courtesy was intact, the voice level as a scale beam. His hands were not. The right one had a band of whiter skin on the third finger where a signet had worn it for thirty years. "The Corn Hall chalked barley at ten this morning. Easing. You'll be pleased."

"Relieved. It isn't the same thing."

They went in. Dain took the chair. Pell, at the ledger bench, found something in the day-book that needed his whole attention.

"My creditors," Dain said, "have found the Lantern Market. Vantheil. Castellane. Two of the grain factors from outside the delta. They're selling my paper under the lanterns at a mark in the crown." He folded his hands. "I paid six marks in the crown for your father's note. You could have the whole of House Dain for less than I paid for a drunk's chandlery."

"I know the price."

"Of course you do." Something moved at the corner of Dain's mouth. "Then you know what comes next. You buy the paper. You go to the Guild Court. My hall, my river frontage, my family's name on a writ nailed to my own door. I came to hear the terms. I thought I owed you the courtesy of hearing them in person, since you afforded me that courtesy in the Deepway."

The rain ticked on the shutters. Lys could see it as a column of figures: the notes, the foreclosure, the hall sold, the plate. Tidy and legal. And every house on the Exchange floor would spend the next ten years making sure Cinderwick could never do it to them. He had learned that once already, over a barge full of stolen barley.

"I'm going to buy the paper," Lys said. "Before someone else does. Then I'm going to restructure it. Twelve years. The Vault's rate, not the Market's. House Dain keeps its hall and its name. You keep your family out of the street."

Dain did not move. "In exchange for what?"

"You." Lys slid a single sheet across the table, still wet at the edges where Pell had blotted it. "You know which of the thirty-nine other Seats will lie to me, and how, and to whom. You know the Guild Court's judges by their debts. I want that in the Company's service. A place at this table. And your name on our charter, as a witness, so that when the Concord houses read it, they read law."

Dain read the sheet. It took him a long time; he was a man who read to the end.

"You want my name," he said, "to make your revolution look like an inheritance."

"I want it to look like what it is. A contract."

"The Seats are few because the sky is small, Master Vey. I told you so. Nothing you've done has made it larger."

"It got larger on Mistmoon 2," Lys said. "About noon. Thirty-one villages paid what they owed, and so did I, and the Stone moved past twenty thousand. Nobody took anything from anyone. You can call that small if you like."

Dain looked up. For a moment the courtesy went out of his face and something older and more tired looked out, and then it was back.

He folded the sheet in half, and in half again, and put it inside the grey coat.

"Ask me again," he said, "after the Guild Court sits on my Seat."

"That isn't no."

"It isn't yes." He rose. At the door he paused, gloves in hand. "Your father would have foreclosed."

"My father would have bought barley," Lys said.

Dain's laugh was a single breath through the nose. He went out into the rain without a hat.

* * *

Veyra was waiting at Mourngate that night, which he had half expected, sitting on a stack of barley sacks with her smoked-glass spectacles pushed up into her silver-streaked hair.

Rain drummed the granary roof. Hobb's men had set pans under the three leaks, and the drip played a slow, uneven tune in the dark. The smell was grain dust and wet rope and lamp oil. There were fewer sacks than there had been in Seedmoon; the covenant carts had taken every one of Harlan's to the poor-houses at four marks, and what stood here now was river grain, worth ten apiece on the slate, easing.

"You made a friend of a ruined man today," Veyra said. "It's a very expensive habit. I approve of it in principle."

"You've been buying his paper."

"Since Harvestmoon 2. At a mark and a half, if you want to know what my foresight cost." She smiled. "Which means that when you buy it, Master Vey, you'll be buying a good deal of it from me."

"At a mark."

"We'll discuss it." She swung her feet. "I'm here about something else. My favour's spent, so call this a bargain. The real one."

"For what?"

"Dain's Seat," Veyra said. "It's forfeit to his creditors. The Guild Court awards a forfeit Seat to a creditor who's a chartered house. The Syndicate holds more of Dain's paper than anyone but you will, once you've bought, and the Syndicate is not chartered. So. You'll stand beside me at the Guild Court. A Sovereign House, sponsoring a new charter. You'll waive your own claim on the Seat, and the Court will give it to the Lantern Syndicate, because no one on those benches will care to tell you no this month."

"You want to come out of the dark."

"I want a lamp that doesn't need a moonless night." She tilted her head. "Is that so surprising?"

It wasn't. She had spent years pricing lots the market had not read yet. This time the lot was herself.

"Conditions," he said.

"Naturally."

"Every shadow note the Syndicate writes, from the day you take the Seat, is registered at the Weigher's counting-house under Sael's witness. Every one. Your book opens on the Temple steps once a year, the way ours did. And the Syndicate bids in its own name. No more proxies in borrowed silks."

"You want to tax my shadows."

"I want to see them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I support whichever of Dain's other creditors has a charter, and you stay under the stair, and I'm sorry, because I'd rather deal with you in daylight than anyone else in Vessant." He meant it, and found that he wanted her to know he meant it, which was a new and unwelcome sensation.

Veyra lowered her spectacles over her eyes. In the lamplight they turned the colour of weak tea and gave nothing back.

"Done," she said. "With one amendment. The twenty per cent on the barges ends the day the Seat is mine. I won't have it said I rode in on your river."

"Done."

She slid off the sacks, shook grain dust from her skirt, and paused at the granary door with the rain behind her.

"Sovereign Houses receive letters, Master Vey," she said. "Old ones. Do read yours before you answer it."

She was gone before he could ask what she meant. He stood among Hobb's pans a long time, and did not like any of the shapes it made.

* * *

Pell came back from Scale Street at noon on Mistmoon 5 with the lot list in his fist and rain running off his nose.

"Next Dawn Auction," he said, and laid it flat on the long table, weighting the corners with an inkpot, a candlestick, a tally-stick, and Hobb's pipe. "Lot 1. I copied it twice to be sure."

Lys read it twice to be sure.

*The Sovereign Charter of the Harrowdales. Lordship of the southern valleys, with rights of rent, levy, and justice. Tier IV. Rebid, the prior holding having lapsed.*

Around the table, nobody spoke. Oren had come from the temple with ink on his cuffs and sat at the end with his thin hands folded. Garrow Finch took up one side of the bench by himself. Mira stood, because Mira stood when she wanted to be able to leave.

"Well," Garrow said at last, slow as a barge warping in. "That's you, then. Lord of the valleys. My lads'll want to know if they're to call you *my lord* or keep calling you what they call you now."

"What do they call me now?"

"The counting-house."

"Keep that."

"The valleys have had a lord," Mira said. "Oskel had the rain, and Oskel's steward had the rest, and when the tithe went unpaid the rain stopped and the steward didn't. I rode through thirty-one villages in Seedmoon on my own name. I'll not ride back through them as some lord's reeve."

"Nobody's asking you to."

"Then what are you asking?"

Oren spoke without unfolding his hands. "Justice is in the charter, Lys. Levy. You cannot simply decline to use them. Someone will sit in judgement in those valleys, whether you appoint him or he takes the chair."

"I know."

"And if you don't bid," said Oren, "one of the Seats that remains will."

He did know. Theo had spent sixteen years in rooms where the question was who ends up owning the thing once the debt is cleared. The answer had never once been *nobody*.

He pulled a clean sheet towards him and uncapped the ink.

"Then the Company bids," he said. "Not me. The Company. And first we write down what the Company is."

He wrote while they watched. It went slowly, because every clause had to survive Oren.

Shares in Cinderwick to every member, one for each year of service, the fourteen who had stayed through Seedmoon counted from the beginning. The Wharrow Brotherhood's stake, which Garrow already held, made permanent and heritable. A share-in-common to each of the thirty-one villages, to be held by the village and not by any elder, and voted at the harvest moot. No single hand to hold more than one part in ten of the whole, the guildmaster's included.

"Yours included?" Garrow said.

"Mine especially."

"That's daft. You're the one who knows what he's doing."

"That's why." Lys did not look up. "A man who knows what he's doing is the one you need a rule against."

The Charter Stone's readout to be read aloud on the Temple steps on the first of every month, and copied onto a plate on the guildhall door. Justice in the valleys to sit with a bench of three, one chosen by the villages, one by the Company, and one by the Temple of the Balance.

"The Temple has one steward on Scale Street," Oren said, "and he is behind on his own accounts."

"Then he'll need a clerk." Lys glanced at Pell, who went pink.

Hobb leaned in and put his thick finger on the line about the door. "A plate. What's it made of? Not gold, I hope. They'd have it off the hinges by morning."

"Brass," Lys said.

"Why brass?"

"It's what they make the weights from at the Corn Hall. It doesn't clip. Nobody hoards it. And you can read it in the rain."

Mira had come round the table while he wrote. She read the whole sheet over his shoulder, lips moving as she had over the Stone, and when she reached the end she set her hand flat on the table beside it, the way the woman had set her palms in the mud.

"A realm in brass, then," she said.

Oren witnessed it. Each of them signed, Pell's hand neat, Garrow's enormous, Hobb's a cross with his name written beside it by Pell. Lys signed last. As the ink went into the paper, the hum in the Stone climbed half a tone, and the light came up white.

```text
⟦ CINDERWICK COMPANY — CHARTER STONE ⟧
STANDING: 26,400 (Tier IV — Sovereign House)
SEAT 39 — HELD
GRACE: 1,388 gleams
```

Nobody cheered. Pell entered the figure in the day-book, and blotted it, and turned the book so they could all see the line.

Oren left to take the witnessed copy to the temple before the evening office. He was back within the hour, and he did not come in the way he had gone out. He came in fast, with his hood still up, and he laid a letter on the table beside the charter without a word.

The paper was heavier than vellum and faintly warm. The seal was a hammer raised above a hammer. The writing ran across it in a script that none of them could read. Pell tried it upside down. Garrow held it to the lamp. Lys touched the edge and his sight opened unbidden: one thread, neither gold nor grey, running up through the roof and past the place where the Exchange should be, into the height he had seen from the flagstones.

"It was on the altar of the Balance," Oren said. His voice was not steady. "Nobody put it there. There is a line in the oldest liturgy that I was taught to recite and not to explain. *Above the Exchange, the High Hammer. Where kingdoms are sold.*"

Nobody spoke.

The Oathbrand on Lys's left wrist began to throb, slow as a second pulse.

*Do read yours before you answer it.*

He could not read it. Veyra had known it was coming and had not said what it cost, and whatever it cost, the Company would pay it now, with every name he had written into brass an hour ago.

"Well?" said Mira.

Lys closed his hand over the letter. It was warm all the way through.

"Yes," he said.
